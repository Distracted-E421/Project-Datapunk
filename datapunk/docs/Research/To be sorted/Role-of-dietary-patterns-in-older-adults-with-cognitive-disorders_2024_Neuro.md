Various  dietary  patterns  (DPs)  may  benefit  or  harm  cognitive  status  through  their  components.  Publications 
assessing the impact of DPs on cognitive scores using neuropsychological tests have often led to less promising 
results. Recently, numerous meta-analyses and systematic reviews have utilized neuroimaging to identify more 
subtle  brain-associated  alterations  related  to  cognition. Combining  neuroimaging  methods  with  neuropsycho-
logical assessments could clarify these findings. This umbrella review was conducted to systematically explore 
evidence on the impact of DPs on neuroimaging biomarkers in older adults with cognitive disorders. Scientific 
databases,  including  Scopus,  PubMed,  and  Web  of  Science,  were  comprehensively  searched  from  the  earliest 
available data until May 11, 2024. Out of 89 papers, 15 meta-analyses and systematic reviews were included in 
our umbrella review. These selected papers addressed 27 DPs and their impact on neuroimaging biomarkers. 
Most selected papers were of moderate quality. Studies revealed that greater adherence to the Mediterranean diet 
(MedDiet) correlated with increased cortical thickness, improved glucose metabolism in the brain, and reduced 
amyloid-beta and tau deposition, as evidenced by magnetic resonance imaging and other neuroimaging tech-
niques. Higher adherence to healthy DPs, such as the MedDiet, reduced the risk of Alzheimer’s disease and mild 
cognitive  impairment.  In  contrast,  Western and  high  glycemic  diets  were  associated  with  increased  cognitive 
decline.

1. Introduction

Dementia encompasses a range of cognitive impairments caused by 
medical conditions that affect brain function, leading to impairments in 
memory,  attention,  perception,  and  thinking.  It  results  in  functional 
decline, making it the seventh leading cause of death and a significant 
cause of global dependency and disability (Collaborators et al., 2021; 
Trivedi, 2006). In 2019, the annual global societal costs averaged $23, 
796 per person with dementia (Wimo et al., 2023). Although dementia 
can result from various diseases, the most prevalent type is Alzheimer’s 

disease  (AD)  (Mak  et  al.,  2017).  The  underlying  mechanisms  of  de-
mentia  generally  involve  the  accumulation  of  improperly  folded  pro-
teins, such as amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaques and neurofibrillary tangles seen 
in  AD.  Mixed  brain  pathologies  are  common  in  older  adults  with  de-
mentia,  leading  to  significant  clinical  consequences  (Chin,  2023). 
Notably, symptoms typically arise after significant brain pathology has 
already  developed,  often  years  before  becoming  clinically  apparent 
(Tarawneh and Holtzman, 2012).

While  there  is  currently  no  cure  for  cognitive  disorders,  adopting 
certain dietary patterns (DPs) can help manage and potentially alter the

Rutten, 2017). DPs refer to the overall diet and the combination of foods 
and nutrients consumed habitually. Emerging evidence from epidemi-
ological  research  (Morris  et  al.,  2003;  Nicolia  et  al.,  2015;  Shannon 
et  al.,  2023),  and  randomized  controlled  trials  (RCTs)  (Fortier  et  al., 
2021; Liu et al., 2021; Valls-Pedret et al., 2015) suggests that assorted 
DPs such as the Mediterranean diet (MedDiet), the Dietary Approaches 
to  Stop  Hypertension  (DASH)  diet,  the  Mediterranean-DASH  diet 
Intervention  for  Neurodegenerative  Delay  (MIND),  and  the  ketogenic 
diet  may  offer  neuroprotective  effects,  potentially  reducing  cognitive 
decline (Arora et al., 2023). These patterns are characterized by their 
specific  food  compositions  and  nutrient  profiles,  which  have  been 
studied  for  their  potential  health  benefits,  particularly  in  relation  to 
cognitive  function  and  neuroprotection  (Arora  et  al.,  2023).  Dietary 
modification is also recognized as a potential approach to decrease the 
overall incidence of dementia in the population (Loeffler, 2021; Town-
send et al., 2023). Observational (Zhou et al., 2022), and interventional 
(Salehi et al., 2011) studies indicate that certain food groups common to 
these  diets—including fruits,  vegetables, legumes, whole grains, nuts, 
and  olive  oil—could  enhance  cognitive  functions  (Barberger-Gateau 
et  al.,  2007;  Lu  et  al.,  2021).  Additionally,  specific  nutrients  like  un-
saturated fatty acids and antioxidants have been linked to a lower risk of 
cognitive deterioration (S. Stavrinou et al., 2020). Given the potential 
impact of DPs, it is essential to explore their effects on cognitive health 
and related biomarkers.

Biomarkers are biological markers utilized to measure and evaluate 
physiological and pathological processes, or pharmacological responses 
to  a  therapeutic  intervention.  Neuroimaging  biomarkers  specifically 
refer to measurable indicators that are obtained through neuroimaging 
techniques, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), functional MRI 
(fMRI), positron emission tomography (PET), and Diffusion Tensor Im-
aging  (DTI)  (Young  et  al.,  2020).  Neuroimaging  biomarkers  plays  a 
pivotal role in early dementia diagnosis by identifying key biomarkers 
using  various  techniques  (Hirsch  et  al.,  2015;  Tartaglia  et  al.,  2011; 
Varghese  et  al.,  2013).  Volumetric  MRI  identifies  early  anatomical 
changes in the transentorhinal cortex and hippocampus, which are key 
in  distinguishing  early  AD  from  normal  aging.  fMRI  and  DTI  reveal 
disruptions in brain areas like the bilateral precuneus and corpus cal-
losum, respectively, while molecular imaging shows variable metabolite 
concentrations  in  the  posterior  cingulate  cortex,  indicating  potential 
diagnostic markers for AD and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) (Talwar 
et  al.,  2021).  Neuroimaging  techniques  such  as  MRI,  single-photon 
emission  computed  tomography,  PET,  dopamine  transporter  scan, 
iodine meta-iodobenzylguanidine myocardial scintigraphy, and amyloid 
and tau PET serve as essential biomarkers for differentiating neurode-
generative diseases by identifying specific brain changes. These methods 
effectively  detect  patterns  such  as  cortical  atrophy,  hypometabolism, 
and abnormal protein accumulation, which are critical for diagnosing 
conditions  like  AD  and  dementia  with  Lewy  bodies  (Shimizu  et  al., 
2018). Recent studies have shown that healthy DPs are associated with 
positive changes in neuroimaging markers, indicating potential benefits 
for brain aging and reduced neurodegeneration (Townsend et al., 2022).
Given the causative or preventive roles of DPs in cognitive disorders, 
as well as the existing links between DPs and neuroimaging biomarkers, 
this umbrella review aims to assess the strength of evidence and identify 
gaps regarding the interplay between assorted DPs and neuroimaging 
biomarkers in older adults with cognitive disorders.

2. Method

We conducted an umbrella review of meta-analyses and systematic 
reviews  following  the  guidelines  of  the  Preferred  Reporting  Items  for 
Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) (Moher et al., 2015). 
The methodology of our study was based on the Cochrane protocol for 
overviews of reviews and the Joanna Briggs Institute approach for um-
brella reviews (Aromataris et al., 2014; Jpt, 2008). Our umbrella review 

protocol  was  registered  in  the  International  Prospective  Register  of 
Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO ID: CRD42024546080).

2.2. Eligibility criteria

Papers were selected in accordance with specific criteria, comprising 
the  requirement  for  complete  research  papers  that  present  meta- 
analyses  and  systematic  reviews.  These  papers  focused  on  assessing 
the  impact  of  various  DPs  in  older  adults  with  cognitive  disorders 
considering  the  neuroimaging  biomarkers.  Exclusion  criteria  encom-
passed  case  series,  letters,  commentaries,  literature  reviews,  case  re-
ports,  animal  studies,  and  quasi-experimental  studies.  Our  umbrella 
review followed the PICO criteria, structuring the review around Pop-
ulation (P: older adults with cognitive disorders), Intervention (I: DPs), 
Comparison  (C:  placebo  or  control),  and  Outcome  (O:  neuroimaging 
biomarkers).

2.5. Methodological quality assessment

2.6. Statistical analysis

The  corresponding  author  (H.A)  evaluated  the  methodological 
quality of the chosen meta-analyses and systematic reviews using the 
Assessment of Multiple Systematic Reviews 2 (AMSTAR2) tool, which 
consists of 16 items across 7 critical domains (Shea et al., 2017). The 
AMSTAR2 questionnaire requires evaluators to respond with “No,” “No 
Meta-analysis,”  “Partial Yes,”  or “Yes”. The ultimate quality of the as-
sessments was categorized as “High quality,” “Moderate quality,” “Low 
quality,” or “Critically low quality”. Moreover, the certainty of the evi-
dence in the selected meta-analyses and systematic reviews was assessed 
using the GRADE tool, with eventual quality graded as “High,”  “Mod-
erate,” “Low,” or “Very low” (Guyatt et al., 2008).

In this umbrella study, no new statistical analysis was executed as the 
primary  objective  was  to assess  the  strength of  evidence and  identify 
gaps. The review encompasses 15 studies, including one meta-analysis 
and  14  systematic  reviews  without  meta-analyses.  These  selected  pa-
pers provided a comprehensive assessment of the current evidence.

3.4. Amyloid-beta deposition

Multiple studies have demonstrated the impact of DPs on Aβ depo-
sition, as a significant marker for AD. The study by Hill et al. investigated 
nutrient  intake,  glycemic  indices,  MedDiet  adherence,  and  AD  bio-

3.6. White matter connectivity

Hill  et  al.  highlighted  that  higher  adherence  to  the  MedDiet  is 
associated with larger total and regional brain volumes and higher white 
matter  (WM)  integrity  (Hill  et  al.,  2019).  Jensen  et  al.  found  that  a 
health-aware diet, characterized by increased consumption of fruits and 
reduced intake of meat, eggs, and spirits, is linked to improved global 
WM  connectivity  and  higher  fractional  anisotropy  (FA).  Fish  oil  sup-
plementation, rich in ω-3 fatty acids, also enhances WM connectivity, 
whereas higher serum leptin levels are associated with worse WM con-
nectivity.  Caloric-restricted  diets  increase  functional  connectivity  be-
tween hippocampal and parietal regions, potentially improving memory 
and spatial processing. The MedDiet is associated with better WM con-
nectivity  and  larger  volumes  of  both  WM  and  grey  matter  (GM), 
particularly in regions like the hippocampus and temporal lobe. Diets 
rich  in  fruits  and  vegetables  improve  WM  connectivity  and  enhance 
functional brain network organization, with higher intake of lycopene 
and ω-3 fatty acids providing additional benefits. Both caloric-restricted 
diets  and  resveratrol  supplementation  have  shown  positive  effects  on 
brain connectivity (Jensen et al., 2021).

3.8. White matter hyperintensities

The  findings  on  the  association  between  diet  and  the  presence  of 
White  matter  hyperintensities  (WMH)  are  varied  across  different 
studies. Opie et al. found no significant association between adherence 
to the MedDiet and the presence of WMH (Opie et al., 2013). In contrast, 
Jensen et al. demonstrated that adherence to the MedDiet was associ-
ated with a reduced occurrence of WMH and brain infarcts. Addition-
ally, high consumption of sugary soft drinks and elevated low-density 
lipoprotein (LDL) levels were linked to higher WMH and brain infarcts 
(Jensen et al., 2021). Arnoldy et al. highlighted that adherence to the 
MedDiet  was  associated  with  a  lower WM  hyperintensity  load in  one 
large study, while no significant associations were found with the Dutch 
dietary guidelines, prudent diet, traditional diet, Western DP, processed 
DP, or nutrient biomarker patterns (Arnoldy et al., 2023).

3.9. Total brain, white matter, grey matter volume

Research  has  consistently  shown  a  significant  correlation  between 
different DPs and brain structure, particularly in terms of brain volumes 
and regional brain health. Higher adherence to the MedDiet is linked to 
larger total and regional brain volumes. For instance, Rodrigues et al. 
demonstrated that adherence to the MedDiet correlates with increased 
total and regional brain volumes((Rodrigues et al., 2020)., while Bianchi 
et al. found that higher MedDiet adherence is associated with greater CT 
in AD-vulnerable regions, larger total brain volumes, and less brain at-
rophy  (Bianchi  et  al.,  2021).  Similarly,  Jensen  et  al.  reported  that 
healthier diets, including the MedDiet, correlate with larger WM and GM 
volumes, especially in the hippocampus and temporal lobes. This diet 
also enhances functional connectivity between hippocampal regions and 
parietal areas and is associated with larger GM volumes in the hippo-
campus, cingulate gyrus, entorhinal cortex, and frontal, temporal, and 
parietal lobes. Additionally, the Paleolithic diet shows a positive link to 
higher GM volumes in older adults. In contrast, the western DP, char-
acterized by a high intake of saturated fats, trans fats, and sodium, is 
associated with smaller GM volumes. Prudent DPs, rich in vegetables, 
vitamins,  antioxidants,  and  fish,  consistently  result  in  larger  GM  vol-
umes. For example, larger GM volumes are linked to fruit and vegetable 
intake, although high fruit consumption may sometimes correlate with 
lower GM volumes due to fructose content(Jensen et al., 2021).

Arnoldy et al. highlighted that nutrient biomarker patterns linked to 
inflammation were associated with smaller total brain volumes, while 
those  including  fiber,  antioxidants,  monounsaturated  fatty  acids,  and 
n3,6 PUFAs are associated with larger volumes. GM volume is generally 
positively  associated  with  adherence  to  the  MedDiet,  particularly  in 
regions such as the right hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, poste-
rior  cingulate  cortex,  frontal  and  temporal  cortex,  and  cerebellum. 
Nutrient biomarker patterns involving VB12, VD, VE, and PUFA are also 
linked  to  larger  frontal  and  temporal  cortex  volumes.  In  contrast, 
alcohol-animal  and  high-fat  nutrient  patterns  are  associated  with 
smaller GM volumes. Regarding hippocampal volume, the MedDiet is 
associated with larger volumes in some studies, particularly in subfields 
like  the  dentate  gyrus.  Adherence  to  healthy  dietary  guidelines  also 
correlates with larger total hippocampal volumes. The Paleolithic diet 
has been linked to larger hippocampal volume in participants with type 
2 diabetes, whereas the Western DP is associated with smaller hippo-
campal  volume  in  cross-sectional  analyses.  WM  volume  results  are 
mixed, with some studies showing positive associations with MedDiet 
adherence  and  others  finding  no  significant  associations  with  the 
Western DP (Arnoldy et al., 2023).

Gregory et al. noted that while some findings suggest higher MedDiet 

adherence may increase hippocampal volume, this effect often lessens 
after adjusting for confounders. However, higher MedDiet adherence is 
more consistently linked to lower WM hyperintensity volume, indicating 
a  potential  protective  effect  (Gregory  et  al.,  2023).  Townsend  et  al. 
found no consistent links between MedDiet adherence and changes in 
GMV over time, though lower adherence might be linked to greater total 
brain volume reduction over three years. Adherence to the AHEI-2010 
diet is associated with an increase in total hippocampal volume, while 
following a prudent diet correlates with an increase in left hippocampal 
volume.  Conversely,  adherence  to  a  Western  diet  is  linked  with  a 
decrease in left hippocampal volume (Townsend et al., 2022).

Gauci et al. highlighted that higher adherence to diet quality indices 
such  as  the  Alternative  Healthy  Eating  Index  (aHEI)  is  significantly 
related to larger hippocampal volumes years later. A prudent-style diet, 
emphasizing vegetables, fruits, whole grains, and fish, is associated with 
larger left hippocampal volumes over time. Conversely, a Western DP is 
associated with smaller left hippocampal volume, indicating potential 
adverse effects on this critical brain region. Furthermore, nutrient pat-
terns  high  in  VB12,  VD,  and  zinc  are  significantly  associated  with 
increased GM volume, while patterns high in saturated fat, cholesterol, 
and  sodium  are  negatively  associated  with  GM  volume(Gauci  et  al., 
2022).

Solfrizzi  et  al.  reported  that  individuals  closely  adhering  to  the 
MedDiet have larger overall brain volumes, including total brain, GM, 
and WM volumes, indicating less brain atrophy and potentially reducing 
the  brain’s  age  by  five  years.  In  contrast,  higher  consumption  of  a 
Western DP, characterized by high intake of red meat, processed foods, 
and sugary drinks, is linked to smaller brain volumes, particularly in the 
left hippocampus. Over a four-year period, adherence to a healthy diet 
versus an unhealthy Western diet resulted in a significant difference in 
hippocampal volume. A prudent DP rich in fresh vegetables, fruit, and 
grilled fish, is associated with larger left hippocampal volume, under-
scoring the importance of plant-based foods and lean proteins in main-
taining  hippocampal  health.  Conversely,  an  unhealthy  Western  DP  is 
associated  with  smaller  left  hippocampal  volume,  highlighting  the 
detrimental  impact  of  poor  dietary  choices  on  brain  health  (Solfrizzi 
et al., 2017).

3.10. Glucose metabolism

Rodrigues  et  al.  found  that  low  adherence  to  the  MedDiet  was 
associated with steeper declines in glucose metabolism (GluM) in the 
temporal and posterior cingulate cortices, which are critical for cogni-
tive  function  (Rodrigues et  al., 2020). Similarly, García-Casares et  al. 
observed that lower adherence to the MedDiet was linked to reduced 
GluM  at  baseline  and  a  more  pronounced  decline  over  time 
(García-Casares et al., 2021). Townsend et al. also reported that lower 
MedDiet  adherence  was  associated  with  significant  reductions  in  the 
cerebral  metabolic  rate  of  glucose,  particularly  in  areas  such  as  the 
temporal and posterior cingulate cortices (Townsend et al., 2022). This 
aligns with findings from Arnoldy, who showed that higher adherence to 
the MedDiet was consistently associated with higher GluM, suggesting a 
lower pathological burden. Arnoldy also noted that nutrient biomarker 
patterns  involving  VE,  PUFA,  antioxidants,  fibers,  vitamin  B12,  and 
vitamin D were linked to higher GluM, while a high-fat nutrient pattern 
was associated with lower GluM (Arnoldy et al., 2023).

Gauci  et  al.  supported  these  findings  by  demonstrating  that  a 
nutrient pattern high in VB12 and D, and zinc was significantly posi-
tively associated with greater GluM in the brain, suggesting these nu-
trients  support  better  brain  function  and  health  (Gauci  et  al.,  2022).

found  that  nutrient  patterns  significantly  impact  brain  health.  They 
noted  that  high  levels  of  VB12,  VD,  and  zinc  improve  GluM  and  GM 
volume,  and  reduce  amyloid  deposition.  Patterns  rich  in  VE,  mono-
unsaturated and PUFAs, vitamin A, vitamin C, carotenoids, and dietary

3.11. Brain infarcts

One  study  revealed  that  higher  adherence  to  the  MedDiet  was 
associated with a 21–24% reduction in AD mortality, with a hazard ratio 
(HR)  of  0.76  (95%  CI:  0.65–0.89,  P  = 0.001).  Notably,  those  in  the 
highest adherence tertile experienced a 73% lower mortality risk (HR 
0.27, 95% CI: 0.10–0.69, P = 0.003) and survived an average of 3.91 
years longer. This association remained significant even after adjusting 
for cardiovascular risk factors (HR 0.77, 95% CI: 0.65–0.91, P = 0.002).
Furthermore, only one study investigated the impact of MedDiet on 
MRI-detected  brain  infarcts and  WMH, finding  that  higher  adherence 
was associated with a 9–11% lower risk of MRI-detected infarcts (odds 
ratio  (OR)  0.89,  95%  CI:  0.80–0.99,  P  = 0.04)  (Opie  et  al.,  2013). 
Another study by Jensen et al. supported these findings, indicating that 
adherence  to  the  MedDiet is  associated  with  a  reduced occurrence of 
WMH and brain infarcts. Conversely, high consumption of sugary soft 
drinks and elevated levels of LDL have been linked to higher occurrences 
of  WMH  and  brain  infarcts,  highlighting  the  importance  of  dietary 
choices  in  managing  and  potentially  reducing  the  risk  of  these  brain 
conditions (Jensen et al., 2021).

4. Discussion

4.1. Summary of key findings

The present umbrella review provides a comprehensive evaluation of 
the existing systematic reviews and meta-analytic studies on the asso-
ciation  of  DPs  and  cognitive  disorders  based  on  neuroimaging  bio-
markers. The overall analysis comprised 15 studies examining various 
DPs,  such  as  the  MedDiet,  DASH,  and  Western  diets,  across  different 
countries. Our findings indicated that higher adherence to healthy DPs, 
such as MedDiet, was associated with larger brain volumes, improved 
WM  integrity,  and  lower  Aβ  deposition,  reducing  AD  and  MCI  risk. 
Western  and  high  glycemic  diets  were  associated  with  poorer  brain 
structures  and  increased  cognitive  decline,  while  other  diets,  such  as 
AHEI and prudent diets, showed neuroprotective effects. The following 
mechanisms may lead to reduced Alzheimer’s pathology and enhanced 
cognitive resilience through different DPs. The MedDiet may reduce Aβ 
deposition,  an  early  Alzheimer’s  marker,  by  enhancing  anti- 
inflammatory  pathways  and  modulating  oxidative  stress,  contrasting 
with  high-glycemic  and  junk-food  diets  that  increase  Aβ  plaque

MCI, as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO), is diag-
nosed by the experience of cognitive decline from a previous level of 
functioning, accompanied by objective evidence of impairment in one or

have indicated dietary factors may benefit for cognitive function in MCI 
patients (McGrattan et al., 2018; Valls-Pedret et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 
2021). MedDiet, DASH, and MIND diets are plant-based and enriched 
with vegetables, fruits, nuts, whole grains, healthy fats, and fish, which 
provide  a  source  of  antioxidants,  B  vitamins,  polyphenols,  PUFAs, 
Monounsaturated  fats,  and  Docosahexaenoic  acid  (Duplantier  and 
Gardner, 2021). Higher adherence to a healthy diet such as the MedDiet 
was generally associated with larger brain volumes, higher WM integ-
rity,  and  lower  diffusivity,  which  correlated  with  higher  cognitive 
scores, with specific regions like the dentate gyrus and various cortical 
areas showing positive correlations. However, low adherence was linked 
to  decreased GluM  and  increased amyloid  deposition in  certain brain 
regions  (Rodrigues  et  al.,  2020).  Additionally,  other  studies  showed 
larger GMV in the hippocampus and parahippocampal gyrus, improved 
CT,  better  WM  integrity,  reduced  AB  deposition,  and  higher  GluM, 
thereby lowering the risk of AD (Arnoldy et al., 2023).

AD is a major medical care challenge of our century and the primary 
cause of dementia, which is marked by a progressive cognitive decline in 
older adults and the accumulation of AB and phosphorylated tau pro-
teins  in  the  brain  (Tzioras  et  al.,  2023).  In  2020,  an  estimated  6.07 
million individuals were diagnosed with clinical AD, with projections 
indicating  that  this  number  will  increase  to  13.85  million  by  2060 
(Rajan  et  al.,  2021).  Neuroimaging  studies  have  shown  that 
amyloid-PET is linked to entorhinal activity, predicts tau deposition, and 
associates with cortical thinning, while tau-PET reveals tau deposition 
predicting  brain  atrophy  and  episodic  memory  deficits.  Additionally, 
task-activated and resting-state fMRI indicated changes in hippocampal 
activity and  default mode network connectivity in  MCI and  AD, with 
diffusion  MRI  showing  widespread  WM  changes,  particularly  in  the 
fornix and cingulum. Structural MRI reveals hippocampal volume and 
shape changes in aging and preclinical AD, with Translocator protein 
(TSPO)  PET,  indicating  increased  microglial  activation  and  imaging 
synapses  showing  reduced  SV2A  binding  in  AD  patients,  correlating 
with memory deficits (M´arquez and Yassa, 2019). Higher adherence to 
the MedDiet has been shown to lead to lower AB loads (Hill et al., 2019), 
while low adherence leads to smaller brain volumes and increased AB 
and neurodegeneration (Bianchi et al., 2021). Additionally, a diet high 
in  red  meats  and  fried  foods  increased  inflammatory  factors,  acceler-
ating cognitive decline and AD (Barberger-Gateau et al., 2014).

4.2. Future directions and implications

The  MedDiet  holds  promising  implications  for  AD  prevention  and 
overall brain health. Studies consistently link high MedDiet adherence 
with  lower  Aβ  deposition,  suggesting  a  dietary  role  in  mitigating  AD 
pathology.  Clinicians  could incorporate  MedDiet  recommendations as 
an early intervention for at-risk individuals to potentially delay or pre-
vent AD progression. Researchers should focus on longitudinal studies 
examining nutrient interactions to strengthen these associations, while 
policymakers  could  support  public  health  initiatives  promoting  the 
MedDiet,  particularly  targeting  middle-aged  and  older  adults.  Higher 
MedDiet adherence is also associated with greater CT and improved WM 
integrity, factors crucial for cognitive resilience. Based on these findings, 
clinicians may consider adding dietary counseling to care plans for pa-
tients with MCI or early AD. Future research could explore the specific 
neurobiological mechanisms through which MedDiet nutrients support 
brain structure, while public health campaigns could highlight the role 
of  MedDiet  in  cognitive  protection  to  encourage  wider  adoption. 
Further, a lower load of WMH and larger brain volume, both linked to 

9 

MedDiet adherence, could contribute to delayed cognitive decline. Cli-
nicians might include dietary evaluations in brain health assessments, 
particularly  for  high-risk  patients.  Research  efforts  could  focus  on 
identifying which dietary elements most effectively support WM integ-
rity, and policies subsidizing whole foods and promoting healthier di-
etary choices could improve MedDiet accessibility across populations. 
Lastly,  the  MedDiet’s  positive  impact  on  GluM  presents  a  dietary 
approach to maintaining cognitive function in aging populations. Cli-
nicians could integrate nutrition-focused guidance into care for patients 
with  glucose  dysregulation,  while  researchers  continue  to  investigate 
nutrient  biomarkers  linked  to  glucose  health.  Policymakers  might 
emphasize  nutritional  education  on  the  role  of  diet  in  metabolic  and 
brain  health, particularly  in  populations  with  high  rates of  metabolic 
syndrome  and  AD,  to  promote  long-term  benefits  for  cognitive  well- 
being.

4.3. Strengths and limitations

This  umbrella  review  provides  a  comprehensive  overview  of  pub-
lished meta-analyses and systematic reviews on the association of DPs 
and  their  impact  on  neuroimaging  biomarkers  in  cognitive  disorders. 
Following  PRISMA  guidelines  and  incorporating  the  Cochrane  and 
Joanna  Briggs  Institute  protocols  ensured  a  rigorous  and  systematic 
approach to synthesizing existing evidence. The thorough search strat-
egy, covering multiple data, minimized the risk of overlooking signifi-
cant studies. Additionally, the use of AMSTAR2 and  GRADE tools for 
assessing  methodological  quality  and  evidence  certainty  further  vali-
dated the robustness and reliability of the findings. Our findings provide 
a broad and nuanced understanding of the relationship between diet and 
brain health.

However,  this  umbrella  review  has  some  limitations.  First  is  the 
potential for selection bias, as it relied on existing meta-analyses and 
systematic reviews, which may have had varying inclusion criteria and 
methodological rigor. Second, very recent individual studies might not 
be  included  in  the  meta-analyses  considered.  Third,  the  quality  and 
conclusions of this umbrella review may be influenced by the limitations 
of  the  included  meta-analyses,  which  varied  in  terms  of  question 
framing,  criteria  definition,  comparisons,  and  populations.  Dietary 
exposure was inconsistently measured across original studies, and the 
meta-analyses used different methods for assessing DPs, leading to po-
tential  variability  in  findings.  Moreover,  dietary  studies  face  several 
methodological challenges, including measurement and quantification 
issues,  recall  bias,  misreporting,  misclassification,  and  measurement 
errors.  Another  limitation  of  this  umbrella  review  was  that  only  one 
selected  paper  was  rated  as  high  quality,  although  the  remaining 
included papers were rated as moderate or low quality. Future studies 
should  standardize  methods  for  assessing  adherence  to  DPs,  defining 
cognitive outcomes, and developing international research guidelines. A 
more coherent and uniform methodology is needed, including detailed 
reporting of interventions, participant characteristics, sample sizes, and 
outcomes.  Finally,  longitudinal  studies  that  track  dietary  habits  and 
neuroimaging  biomarkers  using  neuropsychological  data  over  time 
would provide stronger causal evidence of the effects of diet on cognitive 
decline  and  brain  health.  This  approach  would  enable  more  precise 
quantification of the relationship between DPs and cognitive health.

A  key  methodological  challenge  in  this  umbrella  review  was  the 
heterogeneity of the selected studies. The differences in dietary assess-
ment methods, neuroimaging protocols, and participant characteristics 
made  it  challenging  to  directly  compare  and  synthesize  findings. 
Furthermore, the potential for publication bias and the varying quality 
of studies necessitated careful interpretation of the results. Addressing 
these  challenges  in  future  research  will  be  crucial  to  strengthen  the 
evidence base and enhance the consistency of findings in this field.

5. Conclusion

Our umbrella review indicated significant associations between DPs 
and cognitive health, as evidenced by neuroimaging biomarkers. Higher 
adherence to healthy DPs reduced the risk of AD and MCI. In contrast, 
Western  and  high  glycemic  diets  were  associated  with  increased 
cognitive decline. Future studies should focus on standardizing dietary 
and cognitive outcome measures, employing longitudinal designs, and 
developing international research guidelines to better understand and 
quantify the relationship between DPs and cognitive health.
