The sleep spindle-generating thalamo-cortical circuitry supports attention capacity in awake humans and ani-
mals,  but  using  sleep  spindles  to  predict  differences  in  attention  has  not  been  tried  in  either.  Of  the  more 
commonly  examined  cognitive  correlates  of  spindle  occurrence  and  amplitude,  post-sleep  recall,  and  general 
intelligence,  only  post-sleep  recall  had  been  studied  in  dogs,  rats  and  mice.  Here,  we  examined  a  sample  of 
companion dogs (N = 58) for whom polysomnographic recordings and several cognitive tests were performed on 
two occasions each, with a three-month break in-between. Five of the tests were used to extract a factor anal-
ogous  to  human  g  (general  mental  ability).  A  sixth  test  in  the  battery  measured  sustained  attention.  Both 
attention and g-factor scores were linked to higher slow spindle occurrence and absolute sigma power detected in 
polysomnographic recordings over the central electrode. These effects persisted across measurement occasions. 
Higher intrinsic spindle frequency was, in turn, linked to lower g-factor scores but displayed no relationship with 
attention  scores.  The  overlap  in  localization  and  direction  for  the  effects  of  slow  spindle  density  (spindles/ 
minute) and sigma power supports that they tap into the same underlying cognition-relevant aspects of spindling. 
Given  earlier  large  sample  and  meta-analysis  validations  of  sigma  power  as  a  reliable  predictor  of  cognitive 
performance in humans, we thus conclude that the currently handled method for quantifying spindle density in 
dogs  indeed  measures  cognition-relevant  spindle  activity  by  virtue  of  its  agreement  with  the  sigma  power 
alternative.

1. Introduction

In the search for sleep-derived neural correlates of cognitive func-
tion,  the  so-called  sleep  spindles  (named  for  their  spindle-like  shape) 
have enjoyed a great deal of attention (Fernandez and Lüthi, 2020; Fogel 
and Smith, 2011; Ujma, 2018; Ulrich, 2016; Whalley, 2017). These short 
EEG patterns [maximum 6 s long (Jankel and Niedermeyer, 1985)] are 
mostly  observed  during  non-REM  sleep  and  consist  of  a  series  of 
monomorphic waves (Dutertre, 1977). They can be distinguished from 
other periodic activities during non-REM sleep (e.g., delta, slow oscil-
lations) due to their higher intrinsic frequency (waves/second), which 
appears to be conserved across mammals (Iotchev and Kubinyi, 2021) 
within the 9–16 Hz frequency range.

can, for example, result from intelligence and sleep spindles being sha-

humans. This allows us to test if predictions derived from the human 
literature about sleep spindles and intelligence also apply to the dog. 
Specifically,  sleep  spindle  amplitude  (Ujma,  2018)  and  its  derivative 
sigma power (Ujma et al., 2023) were found to be reliable predictors of 
intelligence in humans. Surprisingly, to our knowledge, attention, the 
cognitive process of selectively concentrating on specific aspects of in-
formation, has rarely or  never been studied as a possible correlate of 
sleep  spindle  activity,  although  attention-relevant  oscillations  (alpha 
waves) are generated by the same circuit (Chen et al., 2016). Attention is 
likely  divided  into  sub-functions  (Johnston  et  al.,  1995),  which 
contribute  to  both  learning  and  intelligence.  The  role  of  the 
thalamo-cortical circuit in attentional processes may be specific to early 
pre-cortical  gating  of  relevant  versus  irrelevant  information.  For  this 
function, arguments derive from thalamic functional anatomy (Crabtree 
and Isaac, 2002), the inability to switch attention between conflicting 
cues when the Reticular Thalamic Nucleus (RTN) is impaired (Ahrens 
et al., 2015) and alpha-band phase associations with stimulus detection 
(Busch et al., 2009; Chen et al., 2016).

In the present study, we examine a sample of dogs which have un-
dergone both polysomnographic recordings and attention tests (specif-
ically  for  sustained  attention)  as  part  of  a  larger  cognitive  battery 
evaluation. From the latter, which included tests of memory and prob-
lem solving, we also calculated g-factor scores for the animals, which 
were extensively validated in Bogn´ar et al. (2024b). Polysomnography is 
a combined measure of EEG with other physiological (heart rate, muscle 
tone)  and  behavioral  variables  (eye  movements),  which  we  use  to 
identify sleep stages in the EEG signal offline. We hypothesize that sleep 
spindle activity may be predictive of attentional performance by virtue 
of reflecting the state of the underlying thalamo-cortical connections. 
Specifically, we expect sleep spindles and attention(al performance) to 
correlate  because  both  are  shaped  by  the  thalamo-cortical  gating 
mechanism described in Steriade and Llinas (1988). Previous work in 
the dog supports the role of high sleep spindle occurrence (Iotchev et al., 
2020a, 2017) in associative sleep-dependent learning and low intrinsic 
frequency  (Iotchev  et  al.,  2020b)  in  reversal  learning.  Sleep  spindle 
amplitude, meanwhile, is a reliable predictor of cognitive performance 
in  humans  (Ujma,  2018)  and  higher  in  young,  healthy  dogs  when 
measured over frontal derivations (Iotchev et al., 2019). We also offer 
comparisons with an alternative summary measure of sleep spindle ac-
tivity - absolute power in the sigma frequency range (9–16 Hz), which 
can  be  extracted  from  EEG  without  detecting  and  locating  individual 
spindles but is assumed to reflect their presence (Fernandez and Lüthi, 
2020).  Sigma  power  was  explored  as  an  alternative  because  it  was 
recently  validated  as  a  reliable  predictor  of  cognitive  performance  in 
>3000 human subjects (Ujma et al., 2023).

2.2. Subjects/data

Dogs’ (N = 58) age varied from 7 to 14 years (M ± SD: 10.3 ± 1.6). 
Due to the original focus on cognitive aging trajectories in senior dogs, 7 
years was the minimum age of inclusion for this sample. Of the total 
sample, 56.9 % (33 dogs) were female and 84.5 % (49 dogs) neutered. 
The demographic factors of age and sex were not included in subsequent 

2 

analyzes since no dog was younger than 7 years, and the majority of the 
sample (84.5 %) was neutered.

The dogs attended both EEG and behavior testing twice, with at least 
3 months between the first and second measurements of each. Corre-
sponding measurements of EEG and behavior (e.g., first EEG and first 
behavior) were, on average, within one month distance of each other 
[mean ± SD: 31.2 ± 48.2 days, see also a description in Iotchev et al. 
(2020b)].  The  EEG  data  was  previously  analyzed  in  relation  to  a 
sub-sample  of  the  behavioral  data  which  we  use  here  (Iotchev  et  al., 
2020b). The results of this published work suggest that within 3 months, 
some sleep spindle features like intrinsic frequency change in a behav-
iorally relevant manner (increases in intrinsic frequency were linked to 
worse  reversal  learning).  Likewise,  dogs’  g-factor  scores  can  change 
within this period (Bogn´ar et al., 2024b). This may be specific to older 
animals  who experience  cognitive decline; therefore, it  applies to the 
sample used here because our subjects were older than 7 years of age. 
Because of this, in the present study, we allowed measures of EEG and 
behavior to be at most separated by a month (threshold at < 30 days), 
excluding  larger  distances  in  timing.  Following  this  selection,  the 
average distance between EEG and behavioral measurements was 13.2 
days (± 9 days SD).

2.3. EEG measurements

Measuring polysomnographic data followed a protocol established 
by Kis et al. (2014). All polysomnographic recordings were capturing 
daytime  naps,  occurring  spontaneously  within  a  time  window  of  3, 
minimum 2 h. All dogs had also undergone an adaptation polysomno-
graphic recording prior to study-related recordings of EEG and behavior, 
so  that  any  hypothetical  ’first  night’  effects  [reported  for  dogs  by 
Reicher et al. (2020)] can be excluded to bias the results presented here. 
EEG  was  measured  non-invasively  with  gold-coated  silver  chloride 
surface  electrodes  (Ag/AgCl)  attached  to  the  scalp  with  EC2  Grass 
Electrode Cream (Grass Technologies, USA). Impedance was adjusted to 
below 15 kΩ. EEG was collected and preprocessed with a 30-channel 
headbox  (Flat  Style  SLEEP  La  Mont)  and  HBX32-SLP32-channel  pre-
amplifier (La Mont Medical Inc., USA). In all dogs, we recorded from the 
midline (Fz and/or Cz), referencing against the occipital bone (Pz) with 
a ground electrode placed over the left musculus temporalis. In dogs, Fz 
placement is at the bone-indentation between the eyes, while Cz is at 50 
% of the distance between Fz and Pz. Electrodes F7 and F8 were placed 
on  the  zygomiotica  for  eye-movement  detection.  The  choice  of  the 
reference and ground positions follows the standard established in Kis 
et al. (2014).

2.4. Spindle detection

The  automatic  script  for  sleep  spindle  detection  in  dogs  was  first 
introduced and described in Iotchev et al. (2017) and is closely modeled 
on the criteria validated in human children by Nonclercq et al. (2013). 
Here and throughout all previous uses of the algorithm (Iotchev et al., 
2023; 2020b, 2020a, 2019, 2017) only the non-REM trace was scanned 
for spindles. The method utilizes a two-step selection. In the first step, 
potential  sleep  spindles  were  selected  on  the  basis  of  power  and 
root-mean-square amplitude values calculated from time windows of 0.5 
s. The maximum power was required to be within the 9–16 Hz frequency 
range [this target frequency range was also used in several studies on 
humans  and  mice  (B´odizs  et  al.,  2009;  De  Gennaro  et  al.,  2008;  Kim 
et al., 2015)], while the amplitude had to be above one standard devi-
ation from the total of non-REM signal time windows. In a subsequent 
step,  the  initial  detections  were  used  to  calculate  new  thresholds  for 
frequency and amplitude through what Nonclercq et al. (2013) describe 
as „normal modeling” (2017). Specifically, a maximum likelihood esti-
mation was calculated for the true mean and range (± 2 standard de-
viations) of spindle amplitude and frequency from the amplitude and 
frequency of the initial detections (see also Iotchev et al. (2017) for more

2.5. Extraction of sigma power

Sigma  power  was  extracted  following  a  similar  protocol  for 
frequency-power extraction as recently applied in Iotchev et al. (2023)
and previously Kis et al. (2017) in the dog: the non-REM sleep segments 
were analyzed with shifting time-windows of 4-second length and 50 % 
overlap using the spectrogram function in Matlab, thus obtained sigma 
power values were averaged across time-windows and frequency-bins 
(frequency resolution was set at 0.1 Hz) within the 9–16 Hz definition 
of the sigma range.

2.7. G-factor scores

A canine g-factor score was obtained by using extraction and analysis 
protocols  established  in  the  human  literature  about  deriving  g  with 
factor  analysis  from  correlated  tests  (Bentler,  1990;  Edwards  and 
Bagozzi,  2000;  Lorenzo-Seva  and  Ferrando,  2015)  on  a  set  of  five 
cognitive tests (Persistency, Problem solving success, Memory, One-trial 
learning, and Associative learning). The tests were previously described 
and  detailed  in  Piotti  et  al.  (2017),  Bogn´ar, Szab´o  et  al.  (2024a) and 
Bogn´ar  et  al.  (2021).  The  extraction  of  g-factor  scores  is  detailed  in 
Bogn´ar,  Turcs´an  et  al.  (2024b).  A  higher  g-factor  score  means  better 
performance across all five cognitive tests. The Attention test was con-
ducted together with the five cognitive tests, but was not included in 
calculating the g-factor score Bogn´ar et al. (2024b).

2.8. Statistical analyzes

Behavior  indicative  of  sustained  attention  was  measured  via  two 
subtests, using a social and non-social context, but only variables from 
the non-social subtest possessed enough variance for further analysis. 
The two subtests had different target stimuli: a frisbee moving up and 
down (seemingly) on its own next to a wall (non-social stimulus con-
dition, Fig. 1.) and the experimenter, with their back to the dog, making 
up-down movements as if painting a wall (social stimulus condition). 
Both stimuli were presented for 1 min, and the dog was leashed during 
the task to prevent it from interacting with the stimuli. The owner was 
seated behind the dog and was instructed not to interact with the animal. 
Likewise, the experimenter did not interact with the dog directly during 
the social stimulus condition. The test was also used and described in 
Bogn´ar  et  al.  (2024a,b),  Chapagain  et  al.  (2020),  and  Wallis  et  al. 
(2014).  We  coded  two  variables  in  each  condition  of  the  test,  the 
duration of looking at the stimulus in total (scored on a 0–3 scale: 0: 
almost never looks at the stimulus; 1: looks < 50 % of the time; 2: looks 
≥ 50  %;  3:  looks  nearly  100  %  of  the  time),  and  the  latency  of  first 
looking away from the stimulus after it started moving (also scored on a 
0–3 scale: 0: < 5 s; 1: 5 - 15 s; 2: 15 - 30 s; 3: ≥ 30 s). Both variables coded 
in the social condition had to be excluded because of low variability. 
This ceiling-effect was possibly caused by heightened reward expecta-
tion in the animals which by then had undergone several rewarded tests. 
We calculated an attention score from the two variables of the non-social 
subtest (duration of looking at the stimulus and latency of first looking 
away  from  the  stimulus)  by  taking  the  mean  of  their  standardized 
version (i.e., z score). The mean of the two variables was used instead of 
the two variables separately, for three reasons. First, because the two 
variables were closely correlated with each other (Cronbach’s alpha =
0.817); second, because a composite variable is less affected by random 
and measurement errors than single variables; third, because the com-

Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMM) were used to analyze the 
data from across all available observations, specifying which observa-
tions belong to the same subjects and different measurement instances. 
Each  GLMM  paired  one  of  seven  spindle-related  predictor  variables 
(density  of  slow  and  fast  sleep  spindles,  amplitude  of  slow  and  fast 
spindles, frequency of slow and fast spindles and absolute sigma power 
for  the  entire  9–16  Hz  frequency  range)  with  one  of  our  two  target 
variables  (attention and  g-factor scores)  and separately for each  elec-
trode (Fz, Cz) resulting in a total of 28 GLMMs. Each model also included 
the  terms  measurement  occasion  (1st  and  2nd)  and  the  interaction 
measurement occasion x predictor. The latter is not explicitly reported 
or discussed but used as a validation criterion: analyzes across all ob-
servations  are  only  considered  if  there  is  no  interaction  between  the 
measurement  occasion  and  the  predictor.  For  models with  significant 
interaction,  we  assumed  that  the  predictor  was  not  supported  due  to 
failed internal replication (agreement between measurement occasions). 
Subject ID, breed and intercept were included as a random factors block. 
All  models  used  robust  coefficient  estimation  to  account  for  possible 
violations of model assumptions. At the same time, we also tested out-
liers  for  amplitude  and  sigma  power  according  to  the  2.68  standard 
score threshold [also used previously (Iotchev et al., 2019) for analyzing 
dogs’  sleep  spindle  amplitudes].  However,  since  for  many  frequency 
bands,  EEG  power  follows  heavy-tailed  distributions  (Roberts  et  al., 
2015),  it  was  not  certain  what  should  count  as  an  outlier  for  sigma 
power. We thus report the effects and p-values for both untouched and 
outlier-cleaned sigma power. Finally, this GLMM template was also used 
to test if either non-REM or REM duration (in minutes) is associated with 
either  attention  or  g-factor  scores.  The  latter  raised  the  number  of 
GLMMs  to  36.  For  all  valid  GLMMs  (no  interaction  for  measurement 
occasion x predictor), the Beta coefficients are reported as a measure of 
effect-size along with the p-values. All analyzes were performed with 
SPSS v22.

In the present study, we compared attention and g-factor scores in 
pet  dogs  with  the  animals’  expression  of  sleep  spindles  (density, 
amplitude, and intrinsic frequency). For the first time in dogs, we report 
a quantification of non-REM absolute power for the spindle-typical 9–16 
Hz frequency range (also known as the sigma band) and its association 
with attention and g-factor scores. The detection of discrete sleep spin-
dles and the extraction of sigma power from across the sleep EEG are 
regarded  as  complementary  measures  of  sleep  spindle  activity  in  the 
human literature [reviewed by Fernandez and Lüthi (2020)]. The latter 
recently  received  a  promising  validation  as  a  predictor  of  cognitive 
performance in >3000 human subjects (Ujma et al., 2023). Including 
both  methods  for  measuring  spindle  activity  allowed  us  to  compare 
where conclusions based on each reach agreement and, therefore, likely 
reflect the true contribution of sleep spindle activity to brain function.
Specifically,  we  observed  that  both  slow  (9–13  Hz)  sleep  spindle 
density and absolute sigma power correlated positively with attention, 
which  was,  however,  moderated  by  whether  sigma  power  was 
controlled for outliers. Both variables emerged as predictors of attention 
over the Cz electrode when outlier-cleaned sigma power was considered. 
Both  effects  would  also  have  survived  the  Bonferroni  correction  for 
multiple  comparisons  (threshold  at  P  < 0.00139).  Untouched  sigma 
power, on the other hand, was more strongly linked to g-factor scores 
and this effect was localized to Fz. The results lend some support to the 
conclusion  that  our  established  method  for  sleep  spindle  detection  in 
dogs  and  absolute  sigma  power  probe  the  same  underlying  trait  – 
cognition-relevant spindle activity.

The result of this comparison adds further support for the validity of 
earlier  spindle-cognition  correlates  reported  in  the  dog.  These  invari-
antly  used  the  same  detection  method  [introduced  in  Iotchev  et  al. 
(2017)] that was also applied here. Since different automatic detectors 
(Goldschmied et al., 2021; Warby et al., 2014) are known to diverge in 
their  estimates  for  the  occurrence  of  sleep  spindles,  agreements  with 
sigma  power  on  its  well-established  cognitive  correlates (Ujma  et  al., 
2023)  are  a  promising  avenue  for  resolving  this  uncertainty.  We 
conclude that the method used in dogs is sensitive to the aspects of sleep 
spindle activity that predict cognitive capacity, as previously shown for 
post-sleep recall and here for attention and g-factor scores.

We  found  limited  support  (nominally  significant  at  alpha  < 0.01, 
without  outlier-correction)  for  an  association  between  g-factor  scores 
and sigma power in dogs (Supplementary Fig. S1). This provides a first 
physiological argument for the validity of g-factor scores in this animal, 
given the recent validation of sigma power as related to intelligence in 
humans (Ujma et al., 2023). Only raw sigma power on Fz displayed a 
link with g-factor scores, while outlier-cleaned sigma power on Cz was 
linked to attention scores instead. This may reflect a trade-off between 
precision and statistical power in assessing the effects of sigma power. In 
humans, the effect size of sigma power as a predictor, though significant, 
is small (Ujma et al., 2023). It may, therefore, be that larger samples of 
dogs are required to observe associations that do not vary as a function 
of (not) removing outliers. Together with the behavioral validation ef-
forts  reported  in  Bogn´ar,  Turcs´an  et  al.  (2024b) and  Iotchev  et  al. 
(2024),  the  present  findings  further  fortify  the  recently  proposed 
method (Bogn´ar et al., 2024b) for measuring general intelligence in dogs 
and  lend  credibility  to  the  assumption  that  the  canine  g-factor  is  an 
analogue of human g.

An important observation is that different aspects of sleep spindle 
activity were linked to either attention or g-factor scores. Sleep spindle 
occurrence  was  a  better  predictor  of  attention  scores  than  of  g-factor 
scores, while intrinsic frequency (waves/s within a spindle) was exclu-
sively linked to g-factor scores: a higher intrinsic frequency of fast and

The present findings are a third instance of linking the occurrence of 
slow spindles to cognition in the dog. The EEG recordings used here are 
also different from the ones previously analyzed when reporting an as-
sociation with command learning (Iotchev et al., 2020a, 2017). While 
for  post-sleep recall, this  effect  was  dominant over  Fz  (Iotchev et  al., 
2020a), for attention and g-factor scores, we see it reach significance 
over  both  (Fz  and  Cz)  electrodes.  Intriguingly,  it  was  also  previously 
shown  that  sleep  spindle  density  over  Cz  decreases  with  age  in  dogs 
(Iotchev et al., 2019), suggesting this to be a promising physiological 
marker  for  age-dependent  changes  in  attention  and  intelligence. 
Although fast sleep spindles (of intrinsic frequency ≥ 13 Hz) have been 
receiving a lot of attention in human learning experiments investigating 
the  effects  of  sleep  on  motor  sequence  learning  (Astill  et  al.,  2014; 
Barakat  et  al.,  2011;  Lustenberger  et  al.,  2016;  Tamaki  et  al.,  2009; 
Yordanova et al., 2017), their overall importance for cognition might be 
overstated. A meta-analysis suggests that findings relating fast spindle 
density to cognition might be subject to publication bias (Ujma, 2018). 
Meanwhile,  although  slow  spindles  have  received  less  attention  in 

human work, their role is better supported across species. In mice, it is 
the  slow  spindle  (ca.  8–9  Hz)  which  was  causally  linked  to  memory 
consolidation (or reduced extinction) through optogenetic manipulation 
(Latchoumane et al., 2017). In dogs, the present results add to previous 
work (Iotchev et al., 2020a, 2017), which exclusively implicates slow 
spindles in the animals’ cognitive performance.

While we focus here exclusively on sleep spindles and power in the 
sigma range (9–16 Hz), cognition-relevant sleep processes may involve 
additional  mechanisms  and  their  oscillatory  expression  in  the  EEG 
(Genzel et al., 2014). Beta power during REM was implicated in human 
intelligence  as  well  (Ujma  et  al.,  2017),  while  during  non-REM,  both 
sigma and beta frequency power can increase following motor learning 
(Morin et al., 2008). Our results specifically add to earlier arguments for 
the role of sleep spindles, but this does not preclude a wider scope in the 
future of both dog and human sleep research.

Our results should be interpreted with caution because the surface 
electrodes cannot provide a precise localization of cortical sources, nor 
monitor subcortical structures. However, one plausible causal explana-
tion  for  the  association  observed  here  may  derive  from  thalamic 
mechanisms  involved  in  both  sleep  spindle  generation  and  attention 
(Chen  et  al.,  2016).  Our  task  for  obtaining  attention  scores  likely 
measured sustained attention since we measured the time dogs would 
orient to novel and arousing stimuli. Ascending and activating thalamic 
signals  (observed  during  burst  mode)  may  play  a  role  in  the  initial 
arousal stage of sustained attention (Jagtap and Diwadkar, 2016; Sarter

Diwadkar,  2016).  Importantly,  both  inhibition  of  peripheral  thalamic 
afferents  and  excitatory  rebound  thalamic  firing  are  observed  during 
sleep spindle generation (Steriade and Llin´as, 1988). It is more parsi-
monious to assume, however, that sleep spindle occurrence and atten-
tion capacity are each reflective of the state of the thalamus, rather than 
draw a direct causal link between sleep spindles and attention, or for 
that matter – general intelligence.

The current study faces a number of limitations, some of which were 
also discussed in a previous analysis of the here-used EEG data (Iotchev 
et al., 2020b). There is a variation in the time elapsed between “corre-
sponding” measures of behavior and EEG [see Iotchev et al. for details 
(2020b)],  although  on  average,  the  gap  is  limited  to  one  month 
(threshold set at < 30 days). However, we note that currently, there is no 
objective threshold by which to decide how much time can elapse for 
both measures to still count as corresponding. Importantly, some effects 
did not depend on this correction, with both our reported and pilot re-
sults  (without  correction  for  time  elapsed)  supporting  the  association 
between slow spindle density and sigma power with attention scores on 
Cz. Another drawback relates to uncertainties around the proper anal-
ysis  of  frequency-power,  in  this  case,  sigma  power.  Frequency-power 
tends  to  follow  a  heavy-tailed  distribution  (Roberts  et  al.,  2015), 
which means that extreme values can be observed without being caused 
by measurement error. A common practice, to log-transform EEG power, 
is not universally supported in the literature [see e.g., Sharpley et al. 
(2023)],  and  in  our  pilot  analyses,  log-transformed  sigma  power  dis-
played no predictive utility. We instead compared outlier-controlled and 
untouched  sigma  power,  the  latter  following  the  assumption  that 
extreme values reflect no measurement error but reflect a heavy-tailed 
distribution.  Sigma  power  displayed  an  invariantly  positive  associa-
tion  with  cognition  with  or  without  controlling  for  outliers,  but  the 
localization  of  this  effect  (Fz  versus  Cz  electrode)  and  the  target 
(attention versus g-factor scores) were affected by whether outliers were 
controlled for. The results reported here also need to be generalized with 
caution,  as  due  to  the  requirements  of  the  original  behavioral  study 
focusing on aging, no subject is younger than seven years, which means 
that no subject was younger than “middle age” according to the classi-
fication proposed by Siegal and Barlough (1995) and the effects were 
overall  weak,  on  a  highly  heterogenous  sample  in  terms  of  breed 
composition. Finally, we did not directly test for internal replication out 
of  concern for statistical power; instead, we used the interaction pre-
dictor  x  measurement  occasion  to  approximate  this  question.  In  the 
human literature, significant but modest effects emerge for very large 
samples,  e.g.,  regarding  sigma  power,  which  concerned  over  3000 
subjects (Ujma et al., 2023), suggesting that every measurement occa-
sion  by  itself is  likely  underpowered  for  a  proper  analysis  of  internal 
replication.  While  we  note  that  in  our  pilot  analyzes,  some  of  the 
explored  models  did  reveal  significant  effects  for  each  measurement 
occasion separately (this concerned, for example, slow spindle density 
and attention on Cz), smaller samples still leave room for more uncer-
tainty as to which results are type I versus type II errors [for dog EEG 
data  this  was  previously  discussed  in  Iotchev  et  al.  (2020a)].  Impor-
tantly,  our  models  specified  which  observations  belong  to  the  same 
subjects and different measurement occasions, such that dependencies 
between data points are not ignored in the analysis.

In  conclusion,  sleep  spindle  findings  in  the  dog  converge  on  the 
relevance of the slow subtype (9–13 Hz) for predicting cognitive per-
formance,  specifically  recall  (Iotchev  et  al.,  2020a,  2017),  sustained 
attention and intelligence (shown here), which is also in agreement with 
findings  from  mice  (Latchoumane  et  al.,  2017)  and  some  studies  in 
humans (Clemens et al., 2005; Kuula et al., 2019; Schmidt et al., 2006). 
The two measures of cognition (attention and g-factor scores) may relate 
to  sleep  spindles  on  the  basis  of  the  same  mechanism  –  the  thalamic 
filtering of peripheral input, which accompanies both sleep spindles and 
awake alpha oscillations (Chen et al., 2016).
