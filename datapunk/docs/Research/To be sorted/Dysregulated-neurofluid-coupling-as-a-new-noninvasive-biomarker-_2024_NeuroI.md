Dysregulated neurofluid coupling as a new noninvasive biomarker for 
primary progressive aphasia

Keywords:

Accumulation  of  pathological  tau  is  one  of  the  primary  causes  of  Primary  Progressive  Aphasia  (PPA).  The 
glymphatic system is crucial for removing metabolite waste from the brain whereas impairments in glymphatic 
clearance in PPA are poorly understood. Thus, this study aims to investigate the role of dysregulated macroscopic 
cerebrospinal  fluid  (CSF)  movement  in  PPA.  Fifty-six  PPA  individuals  and  ninety-four  healthy  controls  were 
included in our analysis after excluding those with excessive head motions during the scan. The coupling strength 
between  blood-oxygen-level-dependent  (BOLD)  signals  in  the  gray matter  and  CSF  flow  was  calculated  using 
Pearson  correlation  and  compared  between  the  groups.  Its  associations  with  clinical  characteristics  including 
scores  from  Clinical  Dementia  Rating  (CDR),  Mini-Mental  State  Exam,  Geriatric  Depression  Scale  and  with 
morphological  measures  in  the  hippocampus  and  entorhinal  cortex  were  examined.  PPA  subjects  exhibited 
weaker global BOLD-CSF coupling compared to HCs, indicating impairments in glymphatic function in the pa-
tients (p = 0.01). In the PPA but not HC group, global BOLD-CSF coupling correlated with the CDR scores (p =
0.04) and hippocampal volume (p = 0.009). The observed decoupling between global brain activity and CSF flow 
and its association with symptomatology and brain structural changes in PPA converges with previous reports on 
the same measure in other neurodegenerative diseases. These findings support the potential role of global BOLD- 
CSF coupling as a noninvasive marker for glymphatic dysregulation in PPA.

1. Introduction

Frontotemporal  dementia  (FTD)  is  a  prevalent  neurodegenerative 
syndrome  primarily  affecting  the  frontal  cortex  and  temporal  lobes, 
commonly arising around age 60 (Lashley et al., 2015; Seo et al., 2018). 
FTD  encompasses  distinct  subtypes:  the  behavioral  variant  of  FTD 
(BV-FTD), characterized by behavioral and executive function deficits, 
and  primary  progressive  aphasia  (PPA)  (Gorno-Tempini  et  al.,  2011; 
Rascovsky et al., 2011). While PPA shares common social and emotional 
symptoms  with  BV-FTD,  such  as  apathy  and  repetitive  behaviors,  it 
specifically denotes the gradual loss of linguistic skills within the initial 
two years of symptom onset (Whitwell, 2019).

Similar  to  other  neurodegenerative  diseases  such  as  Alzheimer’s 
Disease (AD), Parkinson’s Disease (PD), the neuropathology of FTD in-
volves  the  accumulation  of  toxic  proteins  like  microtubule-associated 
protein tau and  TAR-DNA-binding protein (TDP) across various brain 
regions  (Bang  et  al.,  2015;  Boeve  et  al.,  2022).  The  accumulation  of 

these  toxic  proteins  is  linked  to  deficiencies  in  glymphatic  clearance 
(Tarasoff-Conway et al., 2015). The glymphatic system plays a crucial 
role  in  waste  clearance  and  brain  immunity  through  lymphatic  fluid 
movement (Lauren & Maiken, 2021). The glymphatic clearance process 
involves the bulk movement of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from the sub-
arachnoid space along periarterial spaces followed by exchange between 
CSF  and  interstitial  fluid  within  the  parenchyma  (Lauren  &  Maiken, 
2021; Yankova et al., 2021). The exchange between interstitial fluid and 
CSF  necessitates  passage  through  the  parenchyma  controlled  by 
aquaporin-4  (AQP-4)  water  channels  of  astrocytes,  ultimately  being 
discharged into the perivenous space along with brain waste products 
(Mader & Brimberg, 2019). Previous studies have demonstrated glym-
phatic dysfunctions in FTD including significantly higher levels of AQP4 
and larger perivascular spaces (Arighi et al., 2022; Moses et al., 2022). 
Moreover, AQP-4 was found positively correlated with tau levels in FTD 
(Arighi et al., 2022). These findings together suggested a link between 
FTD pathogenesis and dysregulation of the glymphatic system.

Advances in MRI techniques have allowed quantifying CSF pulsation 
in absolute units for clinical diagnosis and prognosis (Harrison  et al., 
2018;  Kim  et  al.,  2022).  Recent  utilization  of  the  inflow  effect  in 
resting-state MRI signals has unveiled a coupling relationship between 
CSF flow in the 4th ventricle and the global brain activity during sleep 
(Fultz et al., 2019). Alterations in the BOLD-CSF coupling strength have 
been identified in various neurodegenerative disorders, including Alz-
heimer’s  disease  (F.  Han  et  al.,  2021),  Parkinson  disease  (Feng  Han 
et  al.,  2021),  and  the  behavioral  variant  of  FTD  (Jiang  et  al.,  2023). 
Given  the  shared  pathogenesis  in  the  accumulation  of  toxic  proteins 
between these disorders and PPA, we hypothesized a similar alteration 
in  BOLD-CSF  coupling  in  PPA.  To  test  this  hypothesis,  using  the 
resting-state  fMRI  data  from  the  Frontotemporal  Lobar  Degeneration 
Neuroimaging  Initiative  (FTLDNI)  databases  (http://memory.ucsf.edu 
/research/studies/nifd),  we  compared  the  BOLD-CSF  coupling  be-
tween  PPA  and  healthy  controls  (HCs).  Based  on  prior  evidence,  we 
hypothesized that patients with PPA had weakened brain-CSF coupling 
which would associate with symptom severity as well as key morpho-
logical measures for FTD such as structural changes in hippocampus and 
entorhinal cortex (ERC).

2.3. Data preprocessing

For the resting-state fMRI data, we used the preprocessing pipeline 
from the 1000 Functional Connectomes Project (version 1.1-beta; pub-
licly  available  at  https://www.nitrc.org/frs/?group_id=296).  This 
pipeline included motion correction by 3dvolreg from AFNI, skull strip-
ping  using  3dcalc  from  AFNI,  spatial  smoothing  (full  width  at  half 
maximum (FWHM) = 6 mm), 0.01-0.1Hz band-pass filtering, and linear 
and  quadratic  temporal  trends  removal  (Cox,  1996).  The  first  10 
resting-state fMRI volumes were discarded to allow for the magnetiza-
tion to reach a steady state. We then co-registered fMRI images from the 
subjects to their high-resolution anatomical images (T1-weighted MRI) 
and finally to the 152-brain Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI-152) 
space by linear registration (flirt function from FSL).

2.4. Extraction of the global BOLD signal and the CSF signal

The global BOLD signal was obtained by extracting the mean BOLD 
signal from the gray matter areas, delineated using the Harvard–Oxford 
cortical structural atlases (Fig. 1) after back-transforming the composite 
anatomical mask to the native space, a method that has been commonly 
adopted  by  previous  studies  (Fultz  et  al.,  2019;  F.  Han  et  al.,  2021; 
Williams et al., 2023). For each individual, we first calculated the mean 
time  series  (230  timepoints)  for  gray  matter  areas  which  was  subse-
quently z-scored and represented the global BOLD (gBOLD) activity.

CSF signals were extracted from the bottom slices of the fMRI images, 
positioned near the bottom of the cerebellum across all participants to 
enhance sensitivity to the CSF inflow effect (Fig. 1), as described in prior 
literature  (Fultz  et  al.,  2019).  T1-weighted  structural  images  were 
segmented  into  different  tissue  types  including  CSF,  gray  matter  and 
white  matter  using  vol2brain  toolbox  (Manj´on  et  al.,  2022).  Derived 
individual CSF tissue probability maps were then registered to the cor-
responding  mean  resting-state  functional  images.  The  registered  CSF 
tissue  probability  map  was  binarized  by  applying  a  threshold  of  CSF 
probability > 90%, from which the slice that corresponded to the bottom 
slice of the corresponding functional was selected as the individual CSF 
mask.  To  ensure  signal  quality  the  CSF  mask  was  manually  edited to 
remove voxels with low signal intensity. Moreover, because of the po-
tential misalignment between the structural and functional images that 
occurred  during  co-registration,  we  additionally  included  CSF  voxels 
that were not covered by the thresholded probabilistic mask based on its 
contrast  to  nearby  brain  tissues  at  the  bottom  slice  of  the  functional 
image.  The  differences  between  segmentation-generated  and  edited 
masks for ten representative subjects are illustrated in (Fig. S1, and both 
types of masks can be found at https://osf.io/wxr4n/). Finally, to show 
that  such  manual  editing  did  not  have  significant  influence  on  our 
findings, results derived from analyses that used the original CSF mask 
(without manual editing) are reported in the Supplementary Materials.

2.5. Quantifying coupling strength between the global BOLD signal and 
CSF signal

Mean fMRI signals from the individual gray matter and CSF areas in 
native  spaces,  averaged  and  then  z-scored  ’s  CSF  mask  from  pre-
processed images.

Cross-correlation functions were computed between the global BOLD 
signal  and  the  CSF  signal,  obtained  through  the  aforementioned  pro-
cedures, to assess their coupling, following a methodology similar to a 
prior study (Fultz et al., 2019). These functions depicted Pearson cor-
relation strengths between the global BOLD signal and the CSF signal 
across a range of time lags (-20s to 20s). Correlation strength between 
BOLD and CSF signals at the negative peak for both groups was used to 
quantify  the  strength  of  the  global  BOLD–CSF  coupling  for  each 
participant.

Additionally, similar to the previous study (Fultz et al., 2019), we 
calculated the cross-correlation function between the negative (tempo-
ral) derivative of the global BOLD signal and the CSF signal to confirm 
that CSF signal matched the negative derivative of the BOLD signal. For 
each  brain  region  in  the  atlas,  we  also  calculated  regional  coupling 
strength  using  a  similar  approach  using  the  averaged,  z-scored  time 
series within each region.

intensity  normalization,  and  automatic  brain  tissue  segmentation.

2.7. Statistical analysis

All  the  statistical  analyses  were  conducted  using  R  software  3.6. 
Group  differences  in  demographic  and  clinical  characteristics  were 
examined  using  independent  t-tests  or  chi-squared  tests.  To  compare 
group differences at the negative peak for BOLD-CSF coupling, we used 
linear regression analyses, with group as the main effect and age and 
gender as covariates. Nonparametric permutation tests were utilized for 
the  group  comparsion  (Johnson  &  Richter,  2022;  Nichols  &  Holmes, 
2002).  Specifically,  we  first  calculated  the  estimated  coefficients  of 
group difference. We then created an empirical null distribution of the 
group  effect  by  randomly  redistributing  the  labels  of  all  participants. 
Subsequently,  we  recomputed  the  surrogated  estimated  coefficients. 
This allowed us to calculate a P-value by determining the proportion of 
the observed group effect within the null distribution.

Relations  between  BOLD-CSF  coupling  strength  and  PPA-related 
neuropsychological measures and brain structural changes were exam-
ined  using  Spearman’s  partial  correlation  controlling  for  age  and 
gender. The p-values of correlational tests were corrected using the false 
discovery  rate  (FDR)  p  < 0.05  was  deemed  significant  after  FDR 
correction.

3.2. BOLD-CSF coupling in PPA

As  shown  in  Fig.  2,  both  PPA  and  HC  groups  exhibited  a  robust 
coupling between global cortical activity and CSF flow. The mean BOLD- 
CSF cross-correlation showed a positive peak at time lags ranging from 
(cid:0) 6 to (cid:0) 8s, as well as a negative peak within time lags of 4–6 s for both 
PPA and HC (Fig. 2). Moreover, the cross-correlation function involving 
the negative first-order derivative of the BOLD signal and the CSF signal 
displayed a notable positive peak at time lags of 0 s.

Similar  to  previous  studies  (F.  Han  et  al.,  2021),  we  chose  the 
cross-correlation  strength  at  the  negative  peak  (time  lag  of  4s)  to 
quantify BOLD–CSF coupling for each individual. Linear regression an-
alyses with group as the independent variable controlling for age and 
gender showed that at this lag, individuals with PPA exhibited signifi-
cantly weaker BOLD-CSF coupling compared to the HC group (PPA =
-0.226 ± 0.182, HC = -0.325 ± 0.187, β  = 0.082, p = 0.01 after per-
mutation 1000 times in estimating the effect size. See Figs. 2, Panel C 
and S2).

3.3. Associations between BOLD-CSF coupling and neuropsychological 
measures

Individual  global  BOLD-CSF  coupling  strengths  at  the  4s  negative 
peak were used to explore the association between BOLD-CSF coupling 
and clinical measurements. Our analysis revealed a significant positive 
correlation between global BOLD-CSF coupling and CDR box score in 
PPA (r = 0.281, p = 0.038, pFDR  = 0.09, Fig. 3). Specifically, brain re-
gions in the frontal and temporal lobes exhibited stronger associations 
between  regional  coupling  and  CDR  box  scores  (Fig.  S2,  Panel  D). 
However, we did not find any significant correlations between global 
BOLD-CSF  coupling  and  CDR  language  scores  in  PPA  (p  = 0.65). 
Furthermore, no significant correlations were observed between global 
BOLD-CSF  coupling  and  MMSE  scores  (pHC  = 0.372,  pPPA  = 0.232), 
depression scores (pHC = 0.614, pPPA = 0.535) in PPA or HC groups.

3.4. Association between global BOLD-CSF coupling and brain structural 
changes

We observed that weaker (less negative) global BOLD-CSF coupling 
was correlated with a smaller hippocampus volume percentage in PPA (r 
= (cid:0) 0.32, p = 0.009, pFDR = 0.03, Fig. 4). However, no significant rank 
correlations  were  observed  between  global  BOLD-CSF  coupling  and 
normalized ERC thickness (p = 0.21).

4. Discussion

The present study found reduced BOLD-CSF coupling in individuals 
with PPA compared to the HC group. Additionally, we observed a cor-
relation between the strength of BOLD-CSF coupling and CDR box scores 
in  PPA  but  not  in  HC.  Furthermore,  our  findings  revealed  a  negative 
association  between  BOLD-CSF  coupling  and  hippocampus  volume  in

PPA. These results suggest that BOLD-CSF coupling holds promise as a 
potential noninvasive marker for assessing glymphatic function, which 
may be relevant to dementia pathology.

Despite  the  large  body  of  evidence  suggesting  the  importance  of 
aggregation  of  the  extracellular  toxic  proteins  in  neurodegenerative 
diseases, it has been rarely studied how changes in glymphatic flow is 
involved in FTD (Nedergaard & Goldman, 2020). In AD, ventricular CSF 
clearance was significantly reduced comparing to healthy controls. This 
clearance abnormality is correlated with deposition of amyloid in the 
gray  matter  (Mony  et  al.,  2017).  In  mouse  model,  attenuation  of  the 
glymphatic flow in the brain parenchyma led to accelerated Aβ  accu-
mulation and cognitive impairment (Xu et al., 2015). In light of these 
findings,  we  examined  whether  PPA  patients  had  altered  coupling 
relationship  between  cortical  activity  and  ventricular  CSF  flow,  a 
recently proposed marker for glymphatic clearance (Fultz et al., 2019; 
Feng Han et al., 2021). Consistent with findings in BV-FTD (Jiang et al., 
2023), our study unveiled a reduction in BOLD-CSF coupling strength in 
PPA compared to HCs. Notably, a stronger coupling between the BOLD

However,  the  association  between  CDR  box  score  and  global

Our investigation revealed a significant association between weaker 
global  BOLD-CSF  coupling  and  reduced  hippocampus  volume.  The 
hippocampus,  pivotal  in  cognitive  decline,  serves  as  markers  for  pre-
clinical  dementia  (Feng  Han  et  al.,  2021).  Previous  neuroimaging 
studies  consistently  reported  significantly  smaller  hippocampus  vol-
umes linked to impaired single-word comprehension despite preserved 
episodic memory in PPA (Bocchetta et al., 2021; Moodley & Chan, 2014; 
Richards  et  al.,  2009;  van  de  Pol  et  al.,  2006).  Neuroimaging  in-
vestigations  have  shown  that  intrathecally  delivered  contrast  agents 
initially  progress  into  the  brain  through  the  posterior  circulation, 
particularly in the hippocampus (Nedergaard & Goldman, 2020). These 
collective  findings  suggest  a  close  connection  between  glymphatic 
function  and  alterations  in  the  hippocampus.  Finally,  this  clinically 
meaningful BOLD-CSF coupling measure can be calculated from com-
mon resting-state data as long as the fourth ventricle is covered by the 
imaging  field,  making  it  an  easily  accessible  brain  marker  that  may 
facilitate clinical diagnosis of PPA.

Although this study shed light on dysregulated glymphatic clearance 
in  PPA,  it  is  essential  to  acknowledge  several  limitations.  Firstly,  the 
resting-state fMRI data employs a conventional fMRI sequence, which 
may have restricted our ability to capture fast brain signals associated 
with cardiac and respiratory frequencies. Future investigations utilizing 
faster  time  repetition  sequences  could  offer  enhanced  resolution  to 
comprehensively explore BOLD-CSF coupling. The precise definition of 
CSF voxels based on the location of the bottom slice is critical. In our 
dataset, the automatically generated CSF mask still requires visual in-
spection and editing. The BOLD-CSF coupling strength derived from the 
fully automated CSF mask showed decreased accuracy compared to the 
manually defined mask. Future studies could benefit from targeting the 
fourth ventricle or areas closer to the bottom of the cerebellum to refine 
CSF signal extraction. Secondly, our exclusive reliance on resting-state 
fMRI  data  might  limit  robust  correlation  of  BOLD-CSF  coupling  with 
the accumulation of specific toxic species. Integrating other modalities 
to measure abnormal accumulation alongside coupling strength could 
offer a more comprehensive understanding of their associations. Lastly, 
despite emerging evidence suggesting a relationship between BOLD-CSF 
coupling  and  the  glymphatic  system,  the  connection  remains  largely 
hypothetical. Direct evidence, especially from studies using murine and 
nonhuman  primate  models,  would  be  invaluable  in  solidifying  and 
elucidating  this  relationship  further.  Meanwhile,  further  studies  are 
needed to explore disease-specific regional BOLD-CSF coupling and its 
role  in  disease  progression.  Addressing  these  limitations  in  future 
research  endeavors  could  refine  our  understanding  of  BOLD-CSF 
coupling, its underlying mechanisms, and its relevance to pathological 
processes in PPA.

5. Conclusion

In the study we found reduced BOLD-CSF coupling in PPA which may 
indicate  altered  glymphatic  function.  Meanwhile,  weaker  coupling 
correlated with cognitive decline and structural changes in brain regions 
implicated PPA pathology including hippocampus. These findings sug-
gest  BOLD-CSF  coupling  as  a  potential  noninvasive  biomarker  for 
quantifying glymphatic alterations in PPA.

slow  wave  activity  often  exhibit  lower  memory  scores  and  increased 
gray  matter  atrophy  (Mander  et  al.,  2013).  Slow  wave  oscillation  al-
terations during both sleep and wakefulness have been observed in de-
mentia  conditions  such  as  AD  and  FTD,  potentially  indicating 
compensation  for  reduced  clearance  rates  during  sleep  (Giustiniani 
et al., 2023; Katsuki et al., 2022; Lewis, 2021). Moreover, recent findings 
by  Jiang  et  al.  (2023) highlighted  reduced  BOLD-CSF  coupling  and 
altered glymphatic measures in BV-FTD, such as higher choroid plexus 
volume  and  altered  diffusion  tensor  imaging  along  the  perivascular 
index,  further  confirmed  the  link  between  BOLD-CSF  coupling  and 
glymphatic clearance in FTD. In line with these observations, our find-
ings  suggested  that  altered  BOLD-CSF  coupling  is  involved  in  FTD 
progression.

It is important to note that brain activity and CSF inflow are influ-
enced by various factors, including arterial pulsation (Iliff et al., 2013), 
vasomotion  (van  Veluw  et  al.,  2020),  sensory  stimulation  (Williams 
et al., 2023), hypertension (Mestre et al., 2018). For example, it has been 
shown that low-frequency CSF activities were modulated by changes in 
cerebral blood volume (Yang et al., 2022). In addition to impaired CSF 
production by the choroid plexus (Jiang et al., 2023), reduced CSF flux 
(Attier-Zmudka  et  al.,  2019),  AQP4  dysfunction  (Arighi  et  al.,  2022), 
vascular  factors,  such as  compromised vascular  integrity  (Hase  et  al., 
2019),  heart  pulsation  (Finger,  2017),  may  also  lead  to  glymphatic 
deficits in PPA. These factors should be taken into consideration when 
interpreting the changes in BOLD-CSF coupling.

Our  study  revealed  a  positive  correlation  between  BOLD-CSF 
coupling strength and CDR box scores in PPA, indicating that reduced 
BOLD-CSF coupling strength links to more severe cognitive impairment. 
Feng Han et al. (2021) demonstrated a correlation between CSF coupling 
and decreased MoCA scores in Parkinson’s disease patients. Specifically, 
our findings suggest that the strength of BOLD-CSF coupling correlates 
with  overall  cognitive  decline  rather  than  being  language-specific.
