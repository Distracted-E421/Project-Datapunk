The  left  lateralization  of  language  has  been  attributed  to  hemispheric  specialization  for  processing  rapidly 
changing information. While interhemispheric differences in auditory cortex organization support this view, the 
macrostructure of the entire cerebral cortex has not been thoroughly examined from this perspective. This study 
investigated hemispheric asymmetries in cortical surface area and thickness and their relationship to pronun-
ciation scores from oral reading using the Human Connectome Project Young Adult dataset (N=1113). Heschl’s 
gyrus had the most left-lateralized surface area, while the temporal pole showed the strongest right-lateralization 
in  thickness.  These  areas  correspond  to  the  core  components  of  speech:  sound  and  meaning.  Notably,  their 
structural features were the only ones also yielding a significant correlation with pronunciation scores. Addi-
tionally,  Broca’s  area’s  posterior  region  (pars  opercularis),  involved  in  articulatory  phonological  processing, 
showed leftward lateralization, contrasting with the right-lateralized anterior portions. Left-hemisphere language 
areas were largely thinner and more extended than their right-sided homologs with a larger white-to-gray matter 
ratio.  Cortical  thickness  was  inversely  related  to  surface area.  The  lateralization  of  auditory-related  language 
areas and their structure’s correlation with pronunciation in oral reading supports a genetically based auditory 
foundation for language. A thinner, more efficient cortex with larger surface areas and increased myelination 
likely  underlies  the  left-hemispheric  dominance  of  language.  Thinner,  more  extended  brain  areas  have  been 
linked to more myelination and wider cortical columns and intercolumnar space. This provides the potential for a 
fast  network  of  interconnected,  discrete  information  units  able  to  support  language’s  demands  of  rapid  cate-
gorical processing.

1. Introduction

Although functional and lesion studies have long recognized the left 
hemisphere’s  critical  role  in  language  (Dax,  1865;  Güntürkün  et  al., 
2020), the precise relationship between cortical lateralization and lan-
guage  processing  remains  elusive.  A  proposed  underlying  mechanism 
for the left-hemispheric bias of language is a division of labor, where the 
left hemisphere specializes in fine-grained temporal processing and the 
right  hemisphere  in  spectral  analysis  (Zatorre,  2022;  Zatorre  et  al., 
2002). Cortical studies offer glimpses into a structural mechanism that 
may  support  this  function.  Increased  temporal  auditory  and  frontal 
phonology-related area sizes have been observed in the left hemisphere 
(Kong et al., 2018; Meyer et al., 2014), while cortical thickness appears 
reduced in left-hemispheric language-related areas (Kong et al., 2018). A 
thinner cortex with expanded surface areas in the left hemisphere may 
better  support  discrete  processing  of  rapidly  changing  auditory  infor-
mation,  as  it  has  been  linked  to  greater  myelination,  interareal 

connectivity,  and  neural  columnar  number,  width,  and  spacing.  If 
cortical  columns  are  basic  processing  units  (Rakic,  1988),  their 
myelinated separation and interconnections provide a fast network of 
discrete  information  stations.  This  would  facilitate  swift  categorical 
processing  (Buxhoeveden  et  al.,  2001a;  Harasty  et  al.,  2003;  Seldon, 
1981;  Tamnes  et  al.,  2009),  crucial  for  phonological,  semantic,  and 
syntactic  processing.  Nevertheless,  lateralization  of  cortical  macro-
structure has yet  to be thoroughly examined  in a  large homogeneous 
sample.  Moreover,  although  specialized  studies  have  related  brain 
structural variation to language function (Golestani et al., 2002; Josse 
and  Tzourio-Mazoyer,  2004;  Mårtensson  et  al.,  2012;  Nov´en  et  al., 
2021a; Schremm et al., 2018), there is no extensive investigation on the 
relation between cortical structure lateralization and language behavior. 
With the advent of freely available large-scale MRI datasets such as the 
Human Connectome Project (HCP) (Elam et al., 2021; Van Essen et al., 
2013), more conclusive insights into the healthy brain’s cortical archi-
tecture  are  now  possible.  This  study  examined  the  lateralization  of

1.1. The brain’s language network

Although the current analysis was unbiased, including and correct-
ing for all parcellated brain areas, special attention was paid to regions 
typically  involved  in  language.  At  its  core,  spoken  language  has  two 
sides: sound and meaning (de Saussure, 1916). The neural representa-
tion of the word leg, for example, must minimally include the sounds 
[lεɡ] along with a set of visual, motor, and sensory features that convey 
how legs look, move, and feel (Pulvermüller and Fadiga, 2010). These 
semantic features need to be coordinated and connected to the sound. 
The left auditory cortex in Heschl’s gyrus (HG, also referred to as the 
transverse temporal gyrus) and its surroundings are responsible for speech 
sound  and  conspecific  vocalization  processing  in  humans  and  other 
primates  (Fig.  1)  (Gaucher  et  al.,  2013).  The  auditory  cortex  in  the 
posterior  superior  temporal  lobe  is  connected  via  the  fiber  bundle 
arcuate fasciculus (AF) with pars opercularis of the inferior frontal gyrus 
(IFG),  and  the  supramarginal  gyrus  (SMG),  a  multimodal  integration 
area of the inferior parietal lobe (Catani and ffytche, 2005). Pars oper-
cularis, the posterior part of Broca’s area, is situated just in front of the 
primary motor cortex’s jaw, tongue, and larynx regions and is involved 
in  higher-level  coordination  of  articulation  (Long  et  al.,  2016).  This 
auditory dorsal stream forms associations between motor programs and 
their acoustic and sensory consequences crucial for phonology during 
speech  learning  (Golestani  et  al.,  2011;  Golfinopoulos  et  al.,  2011; 
Nov´en et al., 2021a). It is also important for grammar, another formal 
(non-semantic)  aspect  of  language,  including  sentence  structure  and 
grammatical relations (Friederici et al., 2017).

The brain’s semantic hub, organizing the meaning features of words, 
has been localized to the left temporal pole (Patterson et al., 2007). This 
is a fitting structure for that function, being an association cortex located 
at  the  extreme  temporal  end  of  both  the  visual  and  auditory  ventral 
streams.  Accordingly,  the  auditory  ventral  stream  is  thought  to  map 
sound to meaning (Hickok and Poeppel, 2007). It builds on the primate

1.2. Cortical structure and language

A  previous  large-scale  study  investigating  the  correlation  between 
cortical  gray  matter  volume  and  oral  reading  in  children  presented 
inconsistent results (Langensee et al., 2023). However, cortical volume 
combines thickness and surface area, which are genetically uncorrelated 
(Panizzon et al., 2009), potentially obscuring distinct relations between 
cortical  structure and  behavior. Cortical thickness decreases  with age 
(Plessen et al., 2014; Zhou et  al., 2013). This can partly explain why 
previous studies with different age groups have shown varying results 
regarding  cortical  thickness  lateralization  (Koelkebeck  et  al.,  2014; 
Luders et al., 2005). Throughout maturation, thickness declines across 
most brain regions, except in the posterior temporal and inferior frontal 
areas, particularly in the left hemisphere. This thinning is likely due to 
increased myelination and synaptic pruning, which enhance the cortex’s 
efficiency, especially in primary processing areas (Sowell et al., 2004). 
The exception in higher-level cognitive areas may be due to the benefits 
of  dendritic  branching  and  connectivity  within  cortical  networks  for 
handling complex information (Nov´en et al., 2019). In this vein, intense 
language learning has been observed to increase the cortical thickness in 
left  superior  temporal  and  inferior  frontal  areas  (Mårtensson  et  al., 
2012), and specific phonetic training has increased the cortical thickness 
of pars opercularis (Golestani et al., 2011). A meta-study reported larger 
surface  areas  but  thinner  cortex  in  the  left  hemisphere  for  all  the 
mentioned  language  areas  except  for  the  SMG  and  pars  opercularis, 
which were not clearly lateralized in cortical thickness, and the anterior 
parts of the IFG, which were smaller in the left hemisphere (Kong et al., 
2018). A compensatory relationship might exist between a thinner, more 
efficient and interconnected left-hemisphere cortex (Seldon, 1981) and 
larger surface areas allowing for a greater number of cortical columns 
(Rakic,  1988)  and  intercolumnar  spacing  (Harasty  et  al.,  2003).  A 
stricter, more myelinated columnar structure might be crucial for the 
rapid,  categorical  analysis  required  in  phonological,  semantic,  and 
syntactic analysis. In language perception, for example, the exact voice 
onset time of an alveolar plosive is not relevant, but rather whether it 
renders  the  phonological  category  /t/  or  /d/.  Semantically,  dis-
tinguishing between a ‘cat’ and a ‘dog’ is more significant than the exact 
size  of  the  animal,  while  syntax  focuses  on  word  class  categories. 
However,  this  structure  would  imply  a  negative  correlation  between 
cortical  surface  area and  thickness  lateralization,  which  has  not been 
reported  in  larger  datasets  (Kong  et  al.,  2018).  A  large,  homogenous 
sample  of  young  adults  is  ideal for  testing  this  correlation  as  cortical 
maturation is largely complete, and cognitive decline has not yet begun.
Relationships  between  auditory  skills  and  brain  morphology  have 
repeatedly been found in HG, which hosts the primary auditory cortex. 
HG  is  a  transverse  outfolding  approximately  halfway  along  the  STG, 
located on the superior temporal plane and largely hidden within the 
Sylvian fissure (Ong and Garey, 1990). In line with the general hemi-
spheric organization, the left HG is more sensitive to rapid changes in 
the  time  domain,  whereas  the  right  hemisphere  has  greater  spectral 
resolution and sound localization (Zatorre and Belin, 2001; Zatorre and 
Penhune,  2001).  HG  has  also  presented  considerable  variation,  inde-
pendent of age. Several instances of HG in either hemisphere  are not 
uncommon.  Most  frequent  are  partial  or  full  duplications,  but  multi-
plications up to four have been found (Benner et al., 2017). In the right 
hemisphere, increased gray and white matter volume and the presence

2. The current study

2.1. Hemispheric specialization

Although the hemispheric asymmetry of the auditory cortex has been 
thoroughly examined, the lateral specialization of cortical structure has 
not been broadly investigated across the entire cerebral cortex. If the left 
hemisphere  is  genetically  adapted  for  faster,  more  differentiated  pro-
cessing, its morphology should reflect this. In the auditory cortex, the 
neural substrates of this type of processing have been hypothesized to 
include increased white matter, facilitating faster signaling while pro-
ducing  more  tangential  dendrites.  The  dendrite  orientation  gives  a 
flatter  cortex  with  less  dendritic  overlap  and  neuronal  columns  more 
separated by myelin, enabling discrete processing. This tangential den-
dritic spread and columnar separation are thought to increase area sizes 
(Harasty  et  al.,  2003;  Seldon,  1981).  Therefore,  the  hemispheric 
specialization should be reflected in a thinner cortex in the left hemi-
sphere  associated  with 
surface  areas  and  a  greater 
white-to-cortical gray matter ratio than in the right hemisphere. If lan-
guage entails exceptional functional lateralization building crucially on 
rapid,  categorical  acoustic  information,  HG  would  be  expected  to  be 
more left-lateralized in area size than structures hosting other primary 
sensory regions.

2.2. Cortical structure and language skills

While language depends on rapid processing, it also implies a com-
plex synthesis of information, combining acoustic features into speech 
sounds, syllables, and words, and uniting semantic features in concepts. 
Such  complex  and  “holistic”  processing  is  associated  with  extensive 
dendritic networks of pyramidal cells in cortical layers III and V linked 
to  increased  cortical  thickness  (Elston,  2003;  Harasty  et  al.,  2003). 
Therefore, within the left hemisphere, higher-level processing regions of 
the  perceptual  streams  and  association  areas  are  expected  to  have  a 
thicker cortex (Nov´en et al., 2019). A better-adapted cortical structure 
might also correlate directly with improved linguistic skills. The current 
study used oral reading scores to operationalize language proficiency. 
Dexterous  reading  involves  access  to  both  the  semantic  content  and 
phonological form of words (Gough and Tunmer, 1986). This is reflected 
in the typical involvement of both the anterior temporal lobes and SMG 
and STG in reading (Price et al., 1997; Wilson et al., 2012). The oral 
reading  test  evaluates  the  pronunciation  of  written  words  with 
increasing  difficulty.  Left  lateralization  in  the  surface  area  of  cortical 
regions involved in semantic and phonological processing would allow 
for a more extensive rapid, interconnected network for language pro-
cessing and improve reading performance. Cortical thickness is likely to 
correlate positively with reading task performance in language-related 
regions since relatively complex competence rather than access to pri-
mary sensory information is crucial for reading skills.

The cortical structure and its relation to reading scores were inves-
tigated in the HCP Young Adult dataset, which presents a homogenous 
group  while  being  large  enough  to  allow  for  solid  conclusions.  A 
compilation of the structural measures in this dataset has shown right- 

lateralization of cortical thickness in most brain regions and a spread 
in the results for surface area lateralization (Malekzadeh and Kashani, 
2020). The present study compared cortical surface area and thickness 
between hemispheres and analyzed the laterality index of parcellated 
regions  while  accounting  for  subject  variability,  intracranial  volume, 
gender, age, and multiple comparisons, and examined how these mea-
sures relate to pronunciation proficiency in oral reading. Results were 
interpreted from the perspective of language processing.

3. Methods

3.1. Procedures

Participant details and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) proced-
ures are described in the WU-Minn HCP 1200 Subjects Release Refer-
ence Manual (https://www.humanconnectome.org/storage/app/media 
/documentation/s1200/HCP_S1200_Release_Reference_Manual.pdf). 
All procedures were performed in compliance with relevant laws and 
institutional guidelines and approved by the Washington University in 
St. Louis Institutional Review Board (IRB ID number 201,204,036). The 
privacy  rights  of  the  participants  were  observed,  and  their  informed 
consent  was  obtained  (Elam  et  al.,  2021).  The  study  included  1113 
participants  (ages  22–35;  606  women)  scanned  using  a  customized  3 
Tesla  Siemens  Skyra.  T1-weighted  images  were  acquired  using  a  3D

FreeSurfer 5.3.033 (Dale et al., 1999) with Desikan-Killiany parcellation 
(Desikan et al., 2006). The FreeSurfer implementation is based mainly 
on the detection of gyri and sulci (Fischl et al., 2004). HG is delimited 
anteriorly  and  medially  by  the  first  transverse  temporal  sulcus  and 
posteriorly  by  the  first  full  Heschl’s  sulcus.  The  lateral  boundary  is 
defined  by  the  dorsolateral  margin  of  the  temporal  lobe  between  the 
anterior limits of the first transverse temporal sulcus and Heschl’s sul-

its  boundary  with  the  supramarginal  gyrus.  Medially,  it  includes  the 
entire ventral bank of the lateral sulcus excluding HG (Klein and Tour-

Data are freely available from the HCP (https://www.humanconnecto 
me.org/study/hcp-young-adult).  Behavioral  tests  included  the  Oral 
Reading Recognition Test and the Picture Vocabulary Test from the NIH 
toolbox (Gershon et al., 2013). Results from the Picture Vocabulary Test 
did  not  survive  multiple  comparison  corrections  and  are  not  further 
described. The Oral Reading Recognition Test evaluates the pronunci-
ation  of  written  words  and  letters,  adapting  to  the  participant’s 
performance.

3.2. Statistical analyses

The current study’s statistical analyses utilized FreeSurfer-extracted 
data for cortical volume, surface area, and average thickness of parcel-
lated  regions.  Linear  mixed  models  were  employed  using  R  (R  Core 
Team, 2024) with the lme4 (Bates et al., 2015), lmerTest (Kuznetsova 
et al., 2017), and MuMIn (Barton, 2009) packages, treating region as a 
repeated measure. Surface area models were simplified to linear models 
due to singular fit. Models controlled for gender, age, and intracranial 
volume (ICV), with age and ICV scaled for better fit. Analyses of cortical 
surface area and thickness included the factors hemisphere and region 
(34 regions).

The lateralization index (LI) was calculated as (left-right) / (left+-
right) (Desmond et al., 1995) for each parcellated region (Fig. 2 and 3). 
It was used to minimize the influence of absolute size in lateralization 
calculation. LI was predicted using region and control variables, with 
effect coding to obtain a general estimate of lateralization. Estimated 
marginal  means  (EMMs)  were  tested  against  zero  for  lateralization 
assessment,  and  pairwise  comparisons  identified  the  most  lateralized 
areas. The relationship between brain region and behavioral measures

5.2. Heschl’s gyrus lateralization and auditory processing

HG showed the strongest left-lateralization in surface area of all re-
gions. This lateralization was also associated with a prominent tenden-
cy—albeit with a considerable variance—toward greater folding in the 
left hemisphere. The parcellation’s posterior boundary at the first full 
Heschl’s sulcus likely excluded cases of full multiplications of HG. Even 
so, the lateral asymmetry is evident. Similar patterns have previously 
been  found  particularly  in  speakers  with  left-hemispheric  language 
dominance (Dorsaint-Pierre et al., 2006). In contrast, the regions hosting 
the primary cortex associated with sensory (postcentral gyrus), visual 
(cuneus), and motor (precentral gyrus) functions showed weaker later-
alization. The specific asymmetry of the auditory cortex related to lan-
guage  lateralization  highlights  the  possibility  that  the  brain  is 
genetically adapted to conspecific vocalizations and speech (Kong et al., 
2018; Meyer et al., 2014). The larger surface area in the left hemisphere 
could  be  related  to  more  extended  regions  dedicated  to  analyzing 
rapidly changing auditory information (Schneider et al., 2005; Warrier 
et al., 2009) fundamental to speech sound perception. Primary sensory 
areas  consist  of  koniocortex  with  many  small  layer-IV  granule  cells 
receiving  afferent  connections  from  the  thalamus  and  relatively  few 
pyramidal neurons. The auditory koniocortex corresponds to a thinner 
(Zoellner et al., 2019) and more myelinated central part of the medial 
two-thirds of HG (Ong and Garey, 1990; Wasserthal et al., 2014). The 
thinner  part  of  HG  thought  to  be  the  primary  auditory  cortex  has, 
indeed, been found to be more elongated in the left hemisphere than in

left-over-right asymmetry. On the other hand, outside koniocortex, the 
left hemisphere presents a clearer vertical columnar structure, especially 
for the pyramidal cells of layers III and V. The columnar spacing is wider 
in  the  left  hemisphere,  possibly  giving  room  for  more  synapses  with 
myelinated  fibers,  allowing  for  more  complex  connectivity  likely  un-

third source of increased area is the detected greater cortical folding, 
which,  in  the  left  hemisphere,  has  previously  been  associated  with 
phonetic expertise (Golestani et al., 2011).

capacity for higher-level cognitive tasks, such as phonological process-

5. Discussion

5.1. Surface area and thickness asymmetries

Language regions in the left hemisphere generally had larger surface 
area and thinner cortex compared to their right-sided homologs. This 
supports the hypothesis of left-lateralization of language due to the need 
for  rapid  processing  (Zatorre,  2022;  Zatorre  et  al.,  2002).  A  thinner 
cortex can be related to more myelinated, faster interareal  communi-
cation in line with a previously found average inverse relation between 
cortical  thickness  and  white  matter  (Tamnes  et  al.,  2009).  Although 
neurodegenerative processes are often also related to cortical thinning, 
especially  in  the  anterior  and  inferior  parts  of  the  temporal  lobes 
(Verfaillie et al., 2016), the relatively thinner left hemisphere cortex in 
healthy  young  adults  is  likely  associated  with  more  streamlined  and 
rapid  information  analysis.  In  favor  of  this  interpretation,  the  white 
matter-to-cortical gray matter ratio was larger in the left hemisphere, 
although  with a  moderate  effect  size. Underlying  this cortical  macro-
structure, the left HG and planum temporale have been observed to have 
wider  neural  columns  and  intercolumnar  space  allowing  for  more 
afferent  and  efferent  connections  than  in  the  right  hemisphere 
(Buxhoeveden  et  al.,  2001a;  Seldon,  1981).  The  present  results  also 
displayed an overall negative correlation between surface area size and 
cortical thickness, supporting the idea that a more tangential dendritic 
spread  might  be  a  factor  behind  a  thinner  cortex,  which  drives  the 
expansion of area size with greater intercolumnar space (Harasty et al., 
2003). Studies focusing on the planum temporale have found that the 
increased  columnar  width,  spacing,  and  compactness  in  the  left

5.4. Temporal pole and semantic integration

The  temporal  pole  exhibited  the  greatest  right-lateralization  in 
cortical thickness and had the thickest cortex in both hemispheres. This 
is expected given its role at the temporal end of both the ventral auditory 
and visual streams, integrating high-level multimodal information. The 
left  anterior  temporal  lobe  is,  accordingly,  associated  with  complex 
processes such as semantic categorization and classification (Patterson 
et al., 2007). It was also the only region to show a significant correlation 
with reading task performance in terms of left lateralization of surface 
area.  In  this  structure,  too,  tangential  dendritic  spread  and  columnar 
width and spacing might be factors influencing lateralization of area size 
and  thickness.  A  clear  columnar  organization  has  been  described  for 
parts of the temporal pole. It is also well-connected with other cortical 
association  areas  supporting  its  function  in  semantic  classification 
(Herlin  et  al.,  2021).  Larger  intercolumnar  space  with  more  myelin 
could increase the left temporal pole’s capacity for interconnectivity and 
semantic categorization. A caveat is necessary, however, that the cor-
relation between cortical structure and behavior is an indirect measure, 
unlike functional magnetic resonance imaging or magnetoencephalog-
raphy,  which  can  index  the  direct  involvement  of  a  brain  area  in  a 
behavior.  Combining  fine-grained  structural  and  functional  measures 
can  increase  insight  into  the  functional  subdivision  of  brain  areas 
(Benner et al., 2023).

6. Conclusions

In conclusion, the current findings on cortical structure corroborate 
the hypothesis that language is left-dominant due to its rapid and cat-
egorical nature. The left hemisphere had a larger white-to-gray matter 
ratio. Its language areas were generally thinner and more extended than 
their right-sided homologs, further reflected in an inverse relationship 
between  cortical  thickness  and  surface  size  lateralization.  This  aligns 
with the suggestion that auditory areas in the left hemisphere are more 
myelinated, leading to tangentially spread dendrites driving increased 
columnar  width  and  spacing.  Such  a  cortex—where  myelination  sup-
ports rapid signal transmission and the isolation of discrete information 
units—may  be  crucial  for  the  fast,  categorical  processing  demands  of 
language.

Two regions stood out in the structural and behavioral analyses: the 
left HG, a key region for auditory and phonological processing, and the 
left temporal pole, associated with semantic categorization. These two 
areas  represent  the  core  properties  of  words:  their  sound  and  their 
meaning.  The  fact  that  they  are  highly  associated  with  acoustic 
perception and the auditory ventral stream favors the idea that speech 
builds on the general primate system for conspecific vocalization pro-
cessing and auditory object recognition. The correlation between brain 
structure and a behavioral language measure is encouraging for future 
research, especially, since the oral reading test is a complex task that 
involves  several  skills:  visual  recognition  of  words,  word  comprehen-
sion, lexical knowledge, reading ability, and pronunciation. Future work 
could investigate more targeted auditory capacities that can be thought 
to  directly  correspond  to  different  functional  regions  of  the  auditory 
cortex.

Lastly, the Desikan-Killiany parcellation is relatively crude. Coming 
research might investigate the cortical structure of sub-parcellations of 
HG and the temporal pole as well as planum temporale and correlate 
their  structure  with  performance  on  targeted  language  tasks.  Recent 
investigations using improved individual segmentation techniques have 
already  shown  a  large  variation  in  the  auditory  cortex’s  size,  shape, 
gyrification (Benner et al., 2023, 2017; Dalboni da Rocha et al., 2020; 
Schneider et al., 2023; Seither-Preisler et al., 2014), and cortical thick-
ness (Zoellner et al., 2019), as well as their correlation with behavioral 
skills. Subdivisions of the temporal pole based on its microstructure and 
potentially relating to its different functions have also been suggested 
(Herlin et al., 2021). As a final note, the fact that pronunciation scores 
correlated with left HG in terms of its cortical thickness, a parameter that 
has  previously  been  found  to  be  to  a  large  extent  experience-driven, 
encourages  auditory  training  programs  for  phonologically  based 
dyslexia (Lohvansuu et al., 2021).
