Cerebellar representation during phonetic processing in tonal and 
non-tonal language speakers: An ALE meta-analysis

The  role  of  the  cerebellum  in  phonetic  processing  has  been  discovered  and  widely  discussed  for  decades. 
However, with the idea that the cerebral representation of phonetic processing is different in tonal language and 
non-tonal  language  speakers,  whether  the  cerebellar  representation  of  phonetic  processing  differs  based  on 
language background remains unknown. In the present study, we conducted an activation likelihood estimation 
(ALE)  analysis  among  33  functional  neuroimaging  studies  involving  541  healthy  adults  (213  tonal  language 
speakers and 328 non-tonal language speakers). The aim was to explore the cerebellar representation of phonetic 
perception  and  phonetic  production  in  these  two  language  backgrounds.  Our  results  demonstrated  the 
involvement of cerebellum left Crus I,  right Crus II, lobules VI, and VIIb in phonetic perception among tonal 
language  speakers,  whereas  only  one  focal  cluster  (right  Crus  I  and  Crus  II)  was  demonstrated  in  non-tonal 
language  speakers.  Conjunction  analysis  revealed  overlapping  regions  located  in  the  right  Crus  II  both  in 
tonal and non-tonal language speakers during phonetic perception. During phonetic production, no significant 
cluster  was  detected  among  tonal  language  speakers,  whereas  one  focal  cluster  (within  right  lobule  VI)  was 
detected in non-tonal language speakers. These results highlight the specific cerebellar representation of phonetic 
processing in tonal and non-tonal languages. Overall, this ALE analysis provides a profound view of the neural 
mechanism of phonetic processing.

1. Introduction

The  functional  representation  of  speech  processing  has  been  dis-
cussed for decades. Speech processing relies on a hierarchical organi-
zation of the speech network, which is centered on the dorsal stream and 
the ventral stream. The dorsal stream contributes mainly to auditory and 
articulatory  phonological  processing  and  is  associated  with  cortical 
representation in the left prefrontal cortex and superior temporal gyrus. 
The  ventral  stream  is  associated  with  conceptual-semantic  processing 
and is distributed mainly in the bilateral temporal and parietal lobes. 
These two streams are formed hierarchically in parallel, with the dis-
covery  that  asymmetrical  recruitment  depends  on  language  tasks 
(Bhaya-Grossman  and  Chang,  2022;  Hickok  and  Poeppel,  2007;  Rau-
schecker  and  Scott,  2009; Yuan  et  al., 2023).  Notably,  most  previous 
studies focused on non-tonal language native speakers, and the latent

Notably, the discussion of language network construction has been 
based mainly on cerebral areas, while the contribution of the cerebellum 
within the language network has rarely been considered. Evidence of 
cerebellar  participation  in  language  processing  has  been  increasingly 
discovered  in  the  last  few  decades.  On  the  one  hand,  structural  con-
nections  between  the  cerebellum  and  the  prefrontal  cortex  lay  a 
fundamental  basis  for  informative  interactions  between  areas  during 
language  processing.  Among  the  cerebrocerebellar  structural  in-
terconnections,  the  cerebellar  Crus  I/II  has  been  demonstrated  to 
communicate with the contralateral cerebral cortex related to language 
processing  through  white  matter  tracts  (Kelly  and  Strick,  2003), 
resulting  in  important  roles  in  cognitive  functions  such  as  attention, 
language, and memory. On the other hand, patients with cerebellar le-
sions demonstrate a series of cognitive dysfunctions, including language 
processing. For example, reduced informative interaction between the 
cerebellum  and  the  supratentorial  language  areas  was  found  among 
patients with autism spectrum disorder during language tasks (D’Mello 
and Stoodley, 2015; Hodge et al., 2010). The impairment of structural 
interconnections  within  the  cerebrocerebellar  loop  was  shown  to  be 
associated with communication problems (Jeong et al., 2012). Among 
patients with post-stroke aphasia (PSA), a recent meta-analysis revealed 
distributed brain regions with abnormal signal responses following PSA 
(Cheng  et  al., 2023). Among the  results of  this meta-analysis,  altered 
spontaneous  activity  within  the  cerebellum  was  also  found,  and  the 
researchers  considered  it  to  be  associated  with  language  dysfunction 
after PSA. In addition to findings of pathological alterations associated 
with  language  disorders,  an  increasing  number  of  functional  neuro-
imaging studies on healthy individuals have shown that the cerebellum 
contributes to various language tasks (Frings et al., 2006; Stoodley et al., 
2012;  van  de  Ven  et  al.,  2020).  However,  not  all  functional  neuro-
imaging  studies  have  demonstrated  cerebellar  involvement  and  cere-
bellar  activation differs  across  studies. This  discrepancy might  be the 
result of the different kinds of language tasks employed in the previous 
studies;  moreover,  the  relatively  small  sample  size  of  the  previous 
neuroimaging studies might make the inference of the contribution of 
the  cerebellum  to  language  function  less  convincing.  Considering  the 
intricate  anatomy  of  the  cerebellum  and  its  potential  contribution  to 
various  cognitive  functions,  identifying  the  contributions  of  specific 
cerebellar  subregions  to  phonetic  processing  and  exploring  the  latent 
differences associated with language culture helps us to provide a pro-
found  view  of  language  network  contributions  (Fig.  1 illustrates

In this study, we conducted activation likelihood estimation (ALE)

speech processing among healthy individuals. This ALE analysis aims to 
identify  cerebellar  regions  associated  with  phonetic  perception  and 
phonetic production and to explore cerebellar language representations 
specific to tonal language and non-tonal language. We hypothesized that 
cerebellum  involves  in  phonetic  processing,  and  distinct  cerebellar 
representations  of  phonetic  perception  and  phonetic  production  are 
specific to language type.

2. Method

2.1. Systematic literature search

We  pre-registered  our  systematic  review  and  meta-analysis  on 
PROSPERO  (ID=CRD42023412492). Functional  neuroimaging  studies 
(positron  emission  tomography  (PET)  and  functional  magnetic  reso-
nance imaging (fMRI)) were searched in the PubMed, EMBASE, and Web 
of  Science  databases.  All  relevant  studies  published  until  30th  April 
2024 were identified through a systematic literature search. Due to the 
potential  underreporting  of  cerebellar  findings,  the  search  did  not 
include the term ‘cerebellum’  but referred to phonetic processing and 
imaging to ensure the identification of all potentially eligible studies. 
The search terms used were ("phonological processing" OR phonological 
OR phonetic OR phonology OR tone OR pitch) AND ("fMRI" OR "func-
tional magnetic resonance imaging" OR "BOLD" OR "PET" OR "positron 
emission tomography"). Additionally, review articles and the reference 
lists  of  the  included  articles  were  checked  to  identify  any  potential 
studies that may have been omitted from the searches.

2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion and exclusion criteria of the included studies were as 

follows:

Inclusion criteria: 

1.  Written in English;
2.  Peer-reviewed experimental studies;
3.  Healthy  monolingual  participants  without  any  hearing,  speaking, 

psychiatric, or neurological disorders;

4. Phonetic  processing-related  tasks  (including  perception  and  pro-
duction)  were  used  in  the  studies  (all  involved  task-activation 
paradigms);

5.  Whole-brain analyses from functional neuroimaging studies on 3D 
coordinates in either the Talairach or Montreal Neurological Insti-
tute (MNI) standardized space were reported;

6.  The results revealed cerebellar involvement.

Exclusion criteria: 

1.  Review studies;
2.  Studies that were non-original or duplicate;
3.  Studies on the elderly population or children;
4.  Regions of interest analysis rather than whole-brain analysis;
5.  Phonetic  processing  task  combined  with  higher-level  cognitive 

component tasks.

The corresponding authors of the studies were contacted to request 
missing or unclear information if the required data were not present or 
ambiguous in the published material. To prevent duplication of data, if 
multiple studies utilized the same data sources, only the article with the 
largest sample size and most comprehensive information was included. 
The study adhered to the guidelines set forth by the preferred reporting

3. Results

2.3. Data extraction

3.1. Systematic search

Two researchers (XZ and ZZ) independently extracted data from each 
study using a predefined data extraction form. Any discrepancies were 
resolved through discussion within the author group. Information on the 
authors,  publication  year,  sample  size,  characteristics  of  the  study 
population  (age,  sex),  and  experimental  paradigm  can  be  found  in 
Table 1. Notably, studies 13 and 14, 31 and 32 were the same study but 
involved different phonetic perception or production tasks or different 
populations. In the present study, we considered them separate studies.

Thirty-three  publications  that  met  the  inclusion  criteria  were 
included  in  the  present  ALE  analysis.  Among  these  studies,  18  were 
related to phonetic perception (11 for tonal language and 7 for non-tonal 
language),  and  15  were  related  to  phonetic  production  (5  for  tonal 
language  and  10  for  non-tonal  language).  A  total  of  541  healthy  in-
dividuals  (213  tonal  language  speakers  and  328  non-tonal  language 
speakers) were enrolled in the present study. The details of the included 
publications can be found in Table 1.

2.4. ALE analysis

3.2. ALE meta-analysis

ALE is a coordinate-based meta-analysis method that evaluates the 
convergence  of  activation  foci  across  different  neuroimaging  studies 
under the premise that the foci reported in the neuroimaging studies are 
spatial probability distributions centered at the given coordinates. The 
resulting maps illustrate the union of activation probabilities for each 
voxel, as the null hypothesis that their convergence is random is rejected 
through a permutation procedure (Eickhoff et al., 2012, 2009; Nichols 
and Hayasaka, 2003; Turkeltaub et al., 2012). Rather than employing a 
pre-specified full-width half maximum (FWHM) for all experiments, the 
probability  distribution  of  each  focus  is  modeled  by  estimating  the 
intersubject and interstudy variability found in standard neuroimaging 
studies  (Lange  et  al.,  2015;  Eickhoff  et  al.,  2012).  ALE  analysis  was 
performed  with  GingerALE  software  (v  3.0.2,  http://brainmap. 
org/ale/).  In  this  study,  all  the  ALE  analyses  were  performed  in  the 
MNI space; therefore, any coordinates reported in the Talairach space 
were  first  converted to  the  MNI  standard  space  prior  to  ALE  analysis 
(Lancaster  et  al.,  2007)  via  the  GingerALE  conversion  tool.  Next,  we 
input the MNI coordinates and sample size for each study into Ginger-
ALE through text files. ALE maps were created using the ALE method. 
ALE values indicate the varying probability of brain activations at each 
voxel. Each reported coordinate was considered the 3D Gaussian prob-
ability distribution center.

The research strategy of the present study was presented in Fig. 3. In 
the  present  study,  we  conducted  6  separate  ALE  analyses.  Two  ALE 
analyses  related  to  phonetic  perception  (n  = 310,  314  foci)  and  pro-
duction (n = 231, 467 foci) were first performed separately, regardless 
of language type (tonal language or non-tonal language). These two ALE 
analyses  were  conducted  to  explore  cerebellar  involvement  during 
phonetic processing on an overall population basis. Four ALE analyses 
were  then  conducted  based  on  language  type,  specifically,  phonetic 
perception  in  tonal  language  speakers  (n  = 166,  199  foci),  phonetic 
perception in non-tonal language speakers (n = 144, 115 foci), phonetic 
production in tonal language speakers (n = 47, 200 foci), and phonetic 
production in non-tonal language speakers (n = 184, 267 foci). For all 
the separate ALE analyses, the cluster-level family-wise error rate was 
set  at  p  < 0.05,  1000  thresholding  permutations,  and  an  intensity 
threshold of p < 0.001. To explore the common cerebellar representa-
tions  of  phonetic  perception  and  phonetic  production  in  both  tonal 
language and non-tonal language speakers, we further conducted two 
conjunction analyses. The resulting maps of the separate ALE analyses of 
phonetic perception and phonetic production, based on language type, 
were  entered  into  GingerALE  for  conjunction  analyses.  The  resulting 
maps were thresholded at p < 0.05 via the false discovery rate without 
assumptions about data cross-correlation (FDR pN).

The  imaging  results  were  displayed  on  surface-based  cerebellar 
flatmaps with the SUIT toolbox (https://www.diedrichsenlab.org/imagi 
ng/suit.htm)  from  SPM12  (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm)  in  the 
MATLAB 2022b environment.

(1) Cerebellar representation of phonetic perception among all in-

dividuals (n = 310, 314 foci)

Cerebellar involvement was found both in phonetic perception and 
regardless  of  language  type.  The  peak  coordinates  of  the  significant 
clusters  were  located  in  the  left  Crus  I  and  the  right  Crus  II  (Fig.  4, 
Table 2).

(2) Cerebellar representation of phonetic production among all in-

dividuals (n = 231, 467 foci)

No significant cluster was identified during the phonetic production 

tasks among all the language speakers (Fig. 4).

(3)  Cerebellar  representation  of  phonetic  perception  in  the  tonal 

language (n = 166, 199 foci)

Three focal clusters within the bilateral cerebellar hemispheres and 
parts of the vermis were identified in the phonetic perception of tonal 
language (left Crus I, right Crus II, lobules VI, and VII). The details are 
shown in Fig. 4 and Table 3.

(4) Cerebellar representation of phonetic perception in the non-tonal 

language (n = 144, 115 foci)

ALE  analysis  in  phonetic  perception  studies  among  non-tonal  lan-
guage speakers revealed one focal cluster in the right Crus I/II, with peak 
voxels located in the right Crus I (Fig. 4, Table 4).

(5)  Cerebellar  representation  of  phonetic  production  in  the  tonal 

language (n = 47, 200 foci)

No  significant  cluster  was  identified  among  phonetic  production 

studies among tonal language speakers in this ALE analysis (Fig. 4).

(6) Cerebellar representation of phonetic production in the non-tonal 

language (n = 184, 267 foci)

One focal cluster within right lobule VI was found in this ALE anal-
ysis (Fig. 4). The peak coordinates of the most significant clusters are 
shown in Table 5.

(7) Conjunction analysis
One focal cluster within the right Crus II was found in conjunction 
with  the  results  of  phonetic  processing  both  in  the  tonal  language 
speakers  and  the  non-tonal  language  speakers  (Fig.  5,  Table  6).  No 
overlapping  cluster  was  found  in  conjunction  analysis  of  phonetic 
production.

Cerebellar  flatmaps  of  significant  clusters  are  color  coded  (the 
cluster-level  family-wise  error  rate  was  set  at  p  < 0.05,  1000  thresh-
olding permutations, and an intensity threshold of p < 0.001).

4. Discussion

In the present study, we performed a coordinate-based ALE analysis 
specific  to  phonetic  processing  of  the  cerebellum  among  healthy 
monolingual adults. Our findings are in part consistent with previous 
findings;  moreover,  we  also  provide  insights  into  the  variability  of 
cerebellar involvement in phonetic processing across different task types 
(phonetic perception and  phonetic production) and language cultures 
(tonal language and non-tonal language).

located in the right Crus II during phonetic perception across all lan-
guage types. For decades, the role of the cerebellum has been considered 
to involve motor function (Friston et al., 1992; Therrien and Bastian, 
2015). However, with the development of neuroimaging techniques, the 
potential contribution of the cerebellum to various cognitive functions, 
including  language  processing,  has  been  demonstrated  (Ashida  et  al., 
2019;  Bernard  and  Seidler,  2014;  Gatti  et  al.,  2021).  A  recent  ALE 
analysis  based  on  403  neuroimaging  studies  revealed  bilateral  cere-
bellum signal response, including the right Crus I/II, lobule VI, left Crus 
I, and lobule VI, across various language processing tasks (Turker et al., 
2023). These regions were also evident in ALE analysis associated with 
phonetic processing. Although in this study, we included only articles 
that specifically reported cerebellar activation, our results are still in line 
with  former  ALE  results,  reflecting  the  importance  of  these  regions 
during phonetic processing. On the one hand, the cerebellum might play 
a  role  in  direct  phonetic  processing  by  communicating  with  supra-
tentorial phonetic networks. From an anatomical perspective, the cer-
ebellum  mainly  connects  with  contralateral  cerebral  regions  through 
white  matter  tracts  (Jansen  et  al.,  2005),  laying  the  foundation  for 
cerebrocerebellar informational interconnections during language pro-
cessing  (Jansen  et  al.,  2005;  Leiner  et  al.,  1991;  Yin  et  al.,  2023).  In 
particular, researchers have demonstrated the direct structural connec-
tion between the cerebellum and cerebral language areas by providing 
streamlines that connect the bilateral Crus lobes and Broca’s complex 
via  tractography  (Zhang  et  al.,  2020).  Moreover,  increasing  evidence 
suggests that the integrity of the cerebrocerebellar white matter loop is 
closely  related  to  cognitive  function  and  that  the  impairment  of  this 
structural  loop  might  in  part  lead  to  various  dysfunctions,  including 
language  impairment  (McKenna  et  al.,  2021;  Radhakrishnan  et  al., 
2023). On the other hand, the cerebellar regions presented in our ALE 
results were also shown to participate in other cognitive functions re-
ported  in  earlier  studies.  For  example,  a  neuroimaging  data  analysis 
from  the  Healthy  Brain  Network  demonstrated  that  the  gray  matter 
volume  of  the  cerebellar  Crus  II  might  be  associated  with  cognitive 
flexibility and that Crus I and lobule VI are related to cognitive control. 
Moreover, Crus I/II and lobule VI are also considered to play a role in 
working memory (B`egue et al., 2023; Guell et al., 2018; Kirschen et al., 
2005). Lobule VI might be a part of ventral attention as well as frontal‒ 
parietal networks (Guell et al., 2018). Although in the present study we 
excluded studies in which the experimental paradigm included a greater 
cognitive  component,  given  the  close  relationship  between  phonetic 
processing  and  other  cognitive  functions  (Acheson  and  MacDonald, 
2009; Meigh et al., 2020), it is still possible that the cerebellum supports 
peri‑linguistic cognitive function during phonetic processing.

In  the  present  study,  we  found  specific  cerebellum  activation  in 
phonetic processing according to language background. During phonetic 
perception, bilateral cerebellar regions were involved (left Crus I, right 
Crus  II,  lobule  VI,  and  lobule  VII)  among  tonal  language  speakers, 
whereas there was only one evident cluster (right Crus I/II) among non- 
tonal  language speakers.  This  result  suggests that  the phonetic  repre-
sentation of the cerebellum might differ depending on language culture. 
In the case of auditory phonetic perception, the cerebral mapping from 
acoustic to phonological representation may vary as a result of distinc-
tions  between  tonal  and  non-tonal  languages.  In  tonal  language,  the 
variation in lexical tone has different meanings, and the tonal discrep-
ancy distinguishes lexical meanings. Moreover, there were also differ-
ences in the representation of the phonology of spoken language across 
different writing systems. The research on non-tonal language included 
alphabetic  languages,  where  graphemes  serve  as  visual  symbols  that 
correspond to phonemes and adhere to grapheme-to-phoneme conver-
sion rules during reading (Tan et al., 2005). The research based on tonal 
language in the current ALE analysis was ideographic language, which 
often  conveys  meaning  through  visual  shapes.  The  abovementioned 
discrepancies result in different cerebral representations. There are re-
sults from previous ALE analyses that support this point. For example,

Veronica et al. reported that the cerebral representations of non-tonal 
auditory lexicon processing and lexical tone processing do not overlap 
exactly  (Kwok  et  al.,  2015).  Moreover,  Tan  et  al.  noted  that  the  left 
middle frontal gyrus is responsible for addressing phonology in Chinese, 
whereas the left temporoparietal areas mediate phonology in alphabetic 
languages  (Tan  et  al.,  2005).  Based  on  the  idea  that  the  cerebellum 
might play a role in phonetic processing through functional interactions 
within the cerebrocerebellar loop (Feng et al., 2017), the found phonetic 
representation of the cerebellum is different based on language back-
ground is reasonable. Additionally, the overlapping cluster in the right 
Crus II among all language types  might indicate a  shared cerebrocer-
ebellar loop involved in phonetic processing.

During phonetic perception, the left Crus I, right lobule VI, and VIIb 
were activated only in the tonal-language speakers. These results might 
be  attributed  to  inevitable  semantic  processing  during  the  phonetic 
perception of tonal language speakers. By exploring neural mechanisms 
among German speakers and Mandarin speakers during tone discrimi-
nation  tasks,  researchers  have  shown  that,  compared  with  German 
speakers, Mandarin speakers exhibit tone processing-evoked temporal‒ 
parietal semantic regions (Chien et al., 2020). Moreover, an increasing 
number of studies have hinted at the role of cerebellar Crus I, lobule VI, 
and VIIb in various semantic tasks (D’Mello et al., 2017; Nakatani et al., 
2023; Reyes-Aguilar et al., 2023). Among patients with language defi-
cits, some studies have also demonstrated a correlation between cere-
bellar alterations and semantic outcomes. For example, decreased gray 
matter volume in the right lobule VI was found in patients with PSA, and 
researchers  also  reported  that  the  strength  of  functional  connectivity 
between the right lobule VI and the left precentral gyrus was correlated 
with comprehension scores among PSA individuals (Zhang et al., 2022). 
A previous ALE study based on structural alterations in frontotemporal 
dementia demonstrated cerebellar changes in the left Crus I and lobule 
VI among semantic dementia patients (Chen et al., 2019). In patients 
diagnosed  with  semantic-variant  primary  progressive  aphasia,  degen-
eration of the right VII lobule was observed (McKenna et al., 2021). We 
should note the variation in task types across different language back-
grounds in the present ALE analysis. A portion of the tasks in the tonal 
language perception condition involved tone discrimination, which was 
not included in the non-tonal language perception condition. On the one 
hand,  during  language  processing,  lexical  access  involves  both 
segmental  information  (consonants  and  vowels)  and  suprasegmental 
information (tone) among tonal language speakers (Malins and Joanisse, 
2012). In  contrast, tonal information  might  not play a  crucial role in 
lexical access for non-tonal language speakers. Thus, the separate ALE 
analysis of phonetic perception specific to language type provide cere-
bellar  representations  associated  with  language  characteristics.  How-
ever,  considering  the  discrepancies  in  tasks  between  language 
backgrounds  in  the  present  study,  future  analysis  should  restrict  the 
included  tasks and  explore the  difference in  cerebellar representation 
between  language  backgrounds  relative  to  similar  psychological 
processes.

With respect to phonetic production, which is specific to language 
type, we failed to discover cerebellar activation among tonal language 
speakers but found cerebellar participation mainly in the right lobule VI 
in the non-tonal language speakers. The role of the cerebellum in pho-
netic  production  has  been  reported  in  previous  studies  (Callan  et  al., 
2007). Researchers have proposed internal models to explain how the 
cerebellum  might  be  involved  in  speech  production.  The  cerebellum 
controls  the  ongoing  temporal  sequencing  of  syllables  during  overt 
speaking  and generates  prearticulatory verbal codes in  the process  of 
covert speech production (Callan et al., 2007). As phonetic production 
relies  on  mapping  phonological  signals  into  articulatory  motor repre-
sentations and produces orderly signals through the precise control of 
muscles  in  articulatory  organs  (Hickok  and  Poeppel,  2007),  such  an 
adaptation role in the cerebellum is important. Specific to lobule VI, it 
has been demonstrated that lobule VI impacts speech-network integra-
tion  and  segregation  (Simonyan  and  Fuertinger,  2015);  meanwhile, 

lobule VI is also considered one part of the salience network associated 
with internal and external stimuli detection and response (Habas et al., 
2009; Sang et al., 2012). An increasing number of studies have shown 
that the left lobule VI plays a role in sensorimotor error processing and is 
used for forward model updating (Fautrelle et al., 2011; Tseng et al., 
2007). This evidence highlights the role of lobule VI as part of the in-
ternal model, which plays a role in phonetic production.

In the present study, we did not find a significant cluster related to 
phonetic production in tonal language speakers. The included studies on 
phonetic production regardless of language type were overt and covert 
readings,  reflecting  the  process  of  mapping  orthography  (print)  to 
phonology (production) (Verhoeven et al., 2017). The cerebellum may 
play  a  role  in  language  production  through  functional  coupling  with 
orthography-selective  areas  in  the  cerebrum.  Based  on  the  finding  of 
different cerebral representations of language production between tonal 
and non-tonal language speakers, the lack of cerebellar representation 
during  phonetic  production  among  tonal  language  speakers  might 
reflect the absence of functional coupling between the cerebellum and 
the  orthography‒phonology  loop  within  the  cerebrum.  However, 
considering the relatively small sample size of phonetic production in 
tonal language speakers (5 articles, 47 individuals included), we could 
not  draw  a  strong  conclusion  in  the  present  ALE  analysis.  Further 
analysis should increase the sample size of tonal language speakers to 
further  explore  the  role  of  the  cerebellum  in  phonetic  production. 
Although  this  study  provided  insights  into  cerebellar  representation 
during  phonetic  processing  specific  to  tonal  and  non-tonal  language 
speakers, we should note that the number of articles included in some 
ALE analyses is relatively small. Given the influence of statistical power 
related  to  sample  size,  further  research  should  explore  cerebellar 
involvement  specific to  language culture with  a  larger sample  size to 
confirm the role of the cerebellum in phonetic processing.
