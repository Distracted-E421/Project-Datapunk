Non-invasive determination of amyloid-β peptide (Aβ) and tau deposition are important for early diagnosis and
therapeutic intervention for Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and non-AD tauopathies. In the present study, we inves-
tigated the capacity of a novel radioiodinated compound AD-DRK (123/125I-AD-DRK) with 50% inhibitory con-
centrations of 11 nM and 2 nM for Aβ and tau aggregates, respectively, as a single photon emission computed
tomography (SPECT) ligand in living brains. In vitro and ex vivo autoradiography with 125I-AD-DRK was per-
formed in postmortem human and two transgenic (Tg) mice lines with either fibrillar Aβ or tau accumulation,
APP23 and rTg4510 mice. SPECT imaging of 123I-AD-DRK was performed in APP23 mice to investigate the
ability of AD-DRK to visualize fibrillar protein deposition in the living brain. In-vitro autoradiogram of 125I-AD-
DRK showed high specific radioactivity accumulation in the temporal cortex and hippocampus of AD patients
and the motor cortex of progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) patients enriched by Aβ and/or tau aggregates. Ex-
vivo autoradiographic images also demonstrated a significant increase in 125I-AD-DRK binding in the forebrain of
both APP23 and rTg450 mice compared to their corresponding non-Tg littermates. SPECT imaging successfully
captured Aβ deposition in the living brain of aged APP23 mice. The present study developed a novel high-
contrast SPECT agent for assisting the diagnosis of AD and non-AD tauopathies, likely benefiting from its af-
finity for both fibrillar Aβ and tau.

1. Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and non-AD tauopathies, such as Pick’s
disease (PiD), progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) and corticobasal
degeneration (CBD) (Virginia M-Y Lee, 2001), are progressive neuro-
degenerative disorders characterized by the accumulation of fibrillar
amyloid-β peptide (Aβ) and/or hyperphosphorylated tau protein
(Alzheimer’s, 2016; Saha and Sen, 2019). Fibrillar Aβ and tau
co-accumulate in cortical and hippocampal regions of AD brain, while
only tau aggregates disease-specifically exist in different brain regions of
non-AD tauopathies (Van Wambeke et al., 2022). Non-invasive detec-
tion of Aβ and tau deposition is crucial for the early diagnosis and
therapeutic intervention of these diseases at a prodromal stage, as
fibrillar Aβ and tau begin accumulating in the brain decades before the
onset of clinical symptoms (Braak, 1991). Non-invasive imaging tech-
nologies exemplified by positron emission tomography (PET) and single
photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) are available for both
preclinical and clinical applications, demonstrating high translational
value. Several PET tracers, such as 18F-florbetapir, 18F-florbetaben, and
18F-flutemetamol, are commercially-available for amyloid imaging
(Cistaro et al., 2018); Tau PET imaging ligands, including 11C-PBB3
(Maruyama et al., 2013), 18F-PMPBB3 (Tagai et al., 2021), 18F-THK5117
18F-THK5351 (Harada et al., 2016),
(Okamura et al., 2013),
18F-MK-6240 (Shuping et al., 2023), 18F-PI-2620 (Mueller et al., 2020)
and 18F-AV-1451 (also known as 18F-T807 or 18F-flortaucepir) (Johnson
et al., 2016), have also been successfully verified in animal models and
patients with AD and non-AD tauopathies.

The FDA has granted accelerated approval to Eisai and Biogen’s
Lecanemab for AD, following the controversial precedent set by Biogen’s
Aducanumab("FDA approves second anti-amyloid antibody for Alz-
heimer disease," 2023). Since amyloid-positive prodromal AD patients
can benefit from such disease-modifying therapy, early screening using
methods like PET for detecting amyloid-positive preclinical and pro-
dromal AD patients becomes essential. Compared to radioisotopes used
in PET imaging, such as 18F (half-life: 110 min), radioisotopes used in
SPECT, exemplified by 123I (half-life: 13.22 h), have a longer half-life.
This allows for longer-distance delivery and lower operating costs.
Additionally, an increasing number of SPECT scanners have been
installed for routine clinical examinations, making SPECT suitable for
primary screening of prodromal AD or non-AD tauopathies patients,
especially in developing countries with large territories. Therefore,
despite the inferior spatial resolution and sensitivity of SPECT compared
to PET, there remains a need to develop SPECT ligands for detecting Aβ
plaques and tau aggregates to aid in medical diagnosis. For the past
decade, several SPECT ligands have been developed for amyloid and tau
imaging, including 123I-DRM106 (Chen et al., 2015), 123I-ABC577 (Maya
et al., 2016) and 125I-BIP (Ono et al., 2016). Among them, only
123I-ABC577 showed its utility for non-invasive imaging of fibrillar Aβ,
but not tau, in patients with AD (Maya et al., 2016). 125I-BIP has shown
in-vitro binding to tau aggregates in temporal lobe of AD brain by
autoradiography, but until now no radioligand has demonstrated suc-
cessful ex-vivo or in-vivo visualization of tau. These tracers exhibit high
binding affinity for fibrillar Aβ or tau. Currently, available tracers are
designed specifically for imaging one type of protein aggregates. There
have been no successful demonstrations of the utility of dual-targeted
ligands that bind both fibrillar Aβ and tau in clinical or preclinical ap-
plications. 18F-FDDNP, a well-known PET ligand, is capable of binding
to both Aβ and tau fibrils (Teng et al., 2011). However, PET imaging
with 18F-FDDNP has shown limited capacity in detecting these fibrils in
the living brains. The distribution volume ratios of 18F-FDDNP are
approximately 1.04 and 1.11 in the frontal cortex of normal controls and
AD patients, respectively (Braskie et al., 2010). Furthermore, Ossen-
koppele et al. reported no significant difference in global cortical
18F-FDDNP binding potential between AD patients and normal controls
(Ossenkoppele et al., 2012). Other dual-targeted probes, such as aste-
mizole and oxindole derivatives, also lack ex-vivo or in-vivo imaging data

to support their capacity to visualize Aβ and tau fibrils in the living brain
(Rojo et al., 2010; Watanabe et al., 2012). The dual-targeted ligand of-
fers two advantages in clinical applications: (1) increased sensitivity for
diagnosing AD by binding to both Aβ and tau (2) the potential for early
diagnosis of non-AD tauopathies. In the present study, we developed a
new compound, named AD-DRK, designed to non-invasively visualize
fibrillar Aβ and tau deposits in the living brains of animal models of
amyloidosis and tauopathy.

2.2. Reagents and antibodies

AD-DRK (5-(5-(6-iodoimidazo[1,2-a]pyridin-2-yl)pyridin-2-yl)oxa-
zole), its radiolabeling precursor and PBB3 were custom-synthesized by
Nard Institute (Kobe, Japan) or Huayi Technology Co. (Suzhou, China).
Commercial antibodies employed in this study are as follows: Mouse
monoclonal antibody against human tau phosphorylated at Ser202 and
Thr205, was purchased from Endogen/Thermo Fisher Scientific (AT8;
Waltham, MA). Mouse monoclonal antibody against the N-terminal
portion (1–16 amino acid residues) of human Aβ (6E10; Signet Labo-
ratories, Dedham, MA).

2.3. Radiosynthesis of radioligands

123/125I-AD-DRK was prepared by the reaction of its precursor with
123/125I-NaI in the presence of chloramine T. 123I-AD-DRK: Briefly,
chloramineT (5µL, 0.4 mg/mL) was added to a reaction mixture (65 µL
of 0.3M phosphate buffer (pH 5.5) + 20 µL of 123I-NaI (92.1 MBq~96.2
MBq; HAT Co., Ltd. (molar activity was adjusted to 115.4 GBq/µmol by
addition of nonradiolabeled NaI) + 20 µL of precursor (1 mg/mL in
MeOH) and incubated for 3 min at room temperature, followed by
quenching by the addition of Na2S2O5 (100 µL, 2.0 mg/mL). The reac-
tion mixture was purified by HPLC, using a SHISEIDO CAPCELLPAK C18
UG120 (φ6.0mm × 150mm; Shiseido, Tokyo, Japan) with a mobile
phase (MeOH/H2O, 60/40, v/v) at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The above
procedure was repeated until 123I-AD-DRK was sufficient for SPECT
imaging. The radioactive fractions were collected and then concentrated
in vacuo. The final radiolabeled product was formulated in saline con-
taining 3.3% (v/v) Tween 80 and 0.8% (v/v) ascorbic acid. The radio-
chemical purity was > 95% at the end of synthesis, and the theoretical
value of the molar activity was 106.5 GBq/μmol, considering that the
reaction of 123I-NaI and precursor led to the formation of 123I-AD-DRK
with a 1:1 stoichiometric ratio and radioactivity decay.

125I-AD-DRK: 125I-AD-DRK was prepared by a similar procedure
mentioned above with slight modification. Briefly, chloramine T (20 μL,
0.10 mg/mL) was added to a mixture of precursor (20 μL, 1 mg/mL in
EtOH), sodium phosphate buffer (70 μL, 0.3 M, pH 5.5), and 125I-NaI
(300 MBq) in a glass vial. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 2 min
at room temperature, and then quenched with aqueous sodium meta-
bisulfite (100 μL, 2.0 mg/mL), followed by purification with the protocol
for 123I-AD-DRK mentioned above. The molar activity and radiochem-
ical purity were 81.4 GBq/μmol and >95%, respectively. The solution
was stored at -20

C until use.

2.8. Small-animal SPECT imaging

2.4. Experimental animals

A transgenic (Tg) mouse line (APP23; strain #:030504), which
overexpresses a mutant form of amyloid-β precursor protein (K670N/
M671L), was purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME).
The rTg4510 mice, tau responder mice, and tetracycline-controlled
transactivator (tTA) mice were obtained from the University of Flor-
ida. A parental mutant tau responder line on an FVB/N strain (Clea Inc.,
Osaka, Japan) and a tTA activator line on a 129+ter/SV strain (Clea
Inc.) were generated and maintained. To generate a tau responder line
expressing human T43 (4-repeat tau isoform without N-terminal inserts)
with P301L mutation, cDNA was placed downstream of a tetracycline-
operon-responder construct. The tTA system was inserted downstream
of the Ca2+
-calmodulin kinase IIα promoter. Hemizygous mice from
each parental line were cross-bred to produce rTg4510 mice possessing
both the responder and tTA constructs, as well as non-transgenic (non-
Tg) littermates (K Santacruz, 2005).

2.5. Biodistribution in normal mice

Normal C57BL/6 mice (male, 8 weeks old) were purchased from
Japan SLC (Hamamatsu, Japan). The mice (n=4 for each time point)
were administered 125I-AD-DRK (0.2 MBq/kg body weight) via the tail
vein and euthanized by cervical dislocation at scheduled time points,
followed by the enucleation of organs of interest. Radioactivity in the
organs was measured by a gamma counter (Wallac Wizard 1480; Per-
kinElmer Wallac Inc, Waltham, USA). The uptake of radiolabelled li-
gands was expressed as a percentage of the injected dose per gram of
tissue (% of ID/g tissue), except for thyroid uptake, which was expressed
as a percentage of injected dose (% of ID).

2.6. In-vitro autoradiographic analysis

Postmortem human brains from an AD patient, a PSP patient and a
healthy control were obtained from the Center for Neurodegenerative
Disease Research at the University of Pennsylvania Perelman School of
Medicine. Fresh-frozen brains were cut into 20-μm-thick sections. The in
vitro autoradiography experiments were conducted as described in our
previous publication (Chen et al., 2014), with slight modifications. Brain
sections were preincubated with PBS for 30 min, followed by incubation
with 125I-AD-DRK (5 nM) or 11C-PiB (5 nM) in PBS at room temperature
for 1 h in the presence or absence of unlabelled AD-DRK (10 μM). After
incubation, the sections were rinsed with PBS twice for 2 min each time
and finally dipped in distilled water for 10 sec. The sections were then
warmly blow-dried and attached to an imaging plate (BAS-MS2025; GE
Healthcare) for contact periods optimized for each experiment. Radio-
labelling was detected by scanning the imaging plate using the
BAS-5000 system (FUJIFILM).

2.7. Ex-vivo autoradiographic analysis

Tg mice and age-matched non-Tg mice (the animal number used
were indicated in the corresponding legends to figures) received a bolus
injection of radioligand (1.1 MBq of 125I-AD-DRK via tail vein. The an-
imals were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and euthanized by
decapitation at predefined time points. The brains were immediately
removed without perfusion, frozen in dry ice, and sectioned into 20-µm-
thick slices using a cryostat microtome (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen,
German). These brain sections were then closely attached to an imaging
plate for an optimized contact period and scanned with the BAS-5000
system. Regions of interest (ROIs) were carefully placed on the indi-
cated brain regions with reference to a brain atlas (The Mouse Brain in
Stereotaxic Coordinates, Paxinos G and Franklin KBJ, Second Edition,
Academic Press, San Diego, 2001).

Three APP23 Tg mice and three age-matched non-Tg mice were
anesthetized with 1.5%-2.0% isoflurane and positioned on the pre-
heated scanner bed of the small animal nanoScan SPECT/CT (Medi-
soMedical Imaging Systems, Hungary). CT imaging was performed for
brain positioning with the following settings: 50 kgvoltage peak X-ray,
980 μA, and 480 projections. Mice were intravenously (i.v.) injected
with a solution of 123I-AD-DRK (37 - 44 MBq in 200 μL saline containing
3.3% (v/v) Tween 80 and 0.8% (v/v) ascorbic acid), followed by an
immediate SPECT scan. Dynamic radio signals were collected continu-
ously for 60 min, with acquisition times sequentially set at 10, 20 and 30
minutes. The energy window for 123I was set at 159 keV ± 20%. Decay
correction factors were calculated from the start of acquisition to a
reference time, defined as the start time of the first acquisition in the first
animal. SPECT acquisition data were reconstructed using Nucline 3.04
software and Tera-Tomo TM 3D SPECT reconstructive algorithm
(MedisoMedical Imaging Systems) with scattering and attenuation
correction. Volumes of interest (VOIs) were placed on the forebrain re-
gions, including the neocortex and hippocampus, and cerebellum of
APP23 and age-matched non-Tg mice in a blinded manner, using PMOD
image analysis software (PMOD Technologies Ltd, Zurich, Switzerland)
with reference to an MRI brain template(Ji et al., 2021).

2.9. Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed as described in our previous
publication using 6E10 (Murray et al., 2011) or AT8 (Ni et al., 2018).
Brain sections were immunostained following a standard protocol with
fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (Molecular Probes/Invi-
trogen). Sections were pretreated with formic acid for antigen retrieval,
and the fluorescent signal was amplified using a tyramide signal
amplification (TSA) kit (Molecular Probes/Invitrogen).

2.11. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses in this study were performed by SPSS soft-

3. Results

3.1. Radiosynthesis of 123/125I-AD-DRK

We successfully synthesized 123I- and 125I-labelled AD-DRK, with
molar activities of 106.5 and 81.4 GBq/μmol, respectively. The radio-
chemical purities of these two ligands were > 95% at the end of syn-
thesis (Fig. 1). 123I possesses excellent physical properties for an imaging
agent, with a photon energy of 159 keV and a half-life of 13 hours. The
gamma emission of 123I enables superior imaging performance (≈80%
efficiency for a ½-inch-thick crystal) with low background activity.
Additionally, it delivers considerably lower radiation doses to the thy-
roid with comparable activity than 131I or 125I. 125I is an artificial
radioactive isotope with a half-life of 59.4 days and emits X-rays and
γ-rays with energies of 27.4–31.5 KeV. Its longer half-life and lower
energy gamma emissions are advantageous for extended and detailed
studies of biological samples. Consequently, 125I is well-suited for ex-vivo
or in-vitro experiments, but not for in-vivo imaging in living subjects
(Comprehensive Handbook of Iodine; February 23, 2009; ISBN-10:
0123741351; Elsevier Academic Press). Therefore,
in the present
study, 125I-AD-DRK and 123I-AD-DRK were utilized for ex-vivo or in-vitro
experiments (such as autoradiography and binding assays) and SPECT
imaging, respectively.

3.2. In-vitro binding affinities for Aβ plaques and tau fibrils

In-vitro binding assay using a one-site binding model demonstrated
that the IC50 values of AD-DRK were approximately 11 nM for Aβ ag-
gregates from APP23 mouse brain homogenates and 2 nM for synthetic
human tau protein (full-length tau-441) fibrils (Fig. 2). Meanwhile, the
previously reported compound DRK092 (Chen et al., 2014), which
shares a similar chemical structure with AD-DRK, exhibits an IC50 value
of >300 nM for tau protein aggregates (SFig. 1). This suggests that
DRK092 is an Aβ-selective agent and does not share the binding site on
tau aggregates with AD-DRK.

3.3. Biodistribution in normal mice

125I-AD-DRK exhibited excellent brain uptake, with an initial peak
value of approximately 7% ID/g, followed by rapid elimination from
normal mouse brains (Table 1). Biodistribution analysis showed that
125I-AD-DRK mainly accumulated in the lungs, kidneys and liver among
peripheral organs in the initial phase. Based on an estimation of no more
than 6% of the injected dose in the kidney and liver at 60 minutes post-
injection, the maximal chemical concentration of the compound was less
than 25 nM, due to the very small amount of administered radioactive
ligand (approximately 0.45 nmol in total). There was no abnormal
accumulation in specific organs and no increase in thyroid uptake
observed during the monitoring period after the bolus injection. These
data suggest negligible concern about either abnormal enrichment in
certain organs or de-iodination in further SPECT imaging for human
subjects.

3.5. Ex-vivo and in-vivo imaging in a mouse model with amyloid
pathology

Ex-vivo autoradiographic images revealed high 125I-AD-DRK accu-
mulation in the forebrain of APP23 Tg mice, which is enriched with
amyloid deposition. The amount of radioactive signal accumulated in
the forebrain was corrected by cerebellar uptake to minimize the in-
fluence of individual differences in arterial input. The radio-signal
accumulation is significantly higher in the forebrain area of 24-month-
old Tg mice than in age-matched non-Tg mice. Slight non-specific
binding of 125I-AD-DRK to white matter was detectable in both Tg and
non-Tg mice at one-hour post-injection, which then decreased to a
negligible level after two hours (Fig. 4A). The target-to-CB binding ratio
at two hours post-injection increased by approximately 55% compared
to that at one hour post-injection (Fig. 4C). Given that longer scan time
results in a decrease in radio-signal, a scan time of no more than two
hours might be optimal for acquiring good contrast with minimum loss
of radioactive signals.

To investigate the capacity for non-invasive detection of amyloid
deposition, we performed in-vivo imaging with 123I-AD-DRK. In APP23
mice, the binding of 123I-AD-DRK in forebrain regions enriched with
amyloid deposition, including the neocortex (CT) and hippocampus
(Hip) was more abundant compared to that in other brain regions, while
no regional differences in radioactivity accumulation were detectable in
age-matched non-Tg mice (Fig. 4B, SFig. 4). The hyperintensive radio-
signal observed around the neck region is attributed to the non-
specific uptake of the compound in the rodent harderian gland (indi-
cated by arrows in Fig. 4B), a gland that is absent in humans. This
phenomenon is commonly observed in PET/SPECT imaging experiments
with rodents. Our previous publication also confirmed such non-specific
accumulation of 11C-PiB and 123I-DRM106 in mice (Chen et al., 2015).
Quantitative analysis of both ex-vivo (Fig. 4C) and in-vivo (Fig. 4D) im-
ages showed significantly higher accumulation of 123I-AD-DRK in amy-
loid pathology-rich regions (cortex/hippocampus) in Tg mice compared
to non-Tg mice.

4. Discussion

The present study has developed a new radioiodinated compound,
123/125I-AD-DRK, which enables the visualization of Aβ and/or tau ag-
gregates in postmortem brains of AD and non-AD tauopathies, as well as
in living brains of animal models. This compound potentially provides a
high cost-performance choice for imaging-based diagnosis of neurode-
generative disorders characterized by Aβ and/or tau aggregates and for
determining applicable human subjects receiving anti-amyloid or anti-
tau therapies.

11C-PIB is an early-developed amyloid-PET tracer. Its application in
amyloid imaging has been well-documented, and therefore has been
widely used in clinical study as a golden standard despite certain limi-
tations. In-vitro binding of 3H-PIB seemed excellent with the binding
ratio of the grey matter to white matter (GM/WM ratio) in postmortem
AD brain being approximately 3.86 (Mukherjee et al., 2021), which is
greatly higher compared to our data. However, we have noticed that
there was 10% alcohol in either incubation or wash buffer. As the
presence of alcohol in incubation or wash buffer can greatly reduce
non-specific binding, particularly in white matter, and thus enhance the
contrast of autoradiographic images, this may explain why in-vitro
3H-PIB binding is approximately 2-fold higher in grey matter than in
white matter even in healthy controls lacking amyloid plaques
(Mukherjee et al., 2021). In contrast, 11C-PiB binding in PET images is
typically higher in white matter than in grey matter in healthy controls

5 

(Nordberg et al., 2010). Additionally, we observed that a new
amyloid-PET tracer, 18F-Flotaza, demonstrated a higher GM/WM ratio
of approximately 40 in male and 80 in female AD patients, respectively
(Sandhu et al., 2024), indicating greater sensitivity with 18F-Flotaza
compared to 11C-PIB. Although in vitro binding to postmortem brain
samples heavily depends on experimental conditions, such as incubation
and wash buffer composition, comparing new tracers with PIB under
identical experimental conditions remains informative for predicting in
vivo performance in AD brains. In-vitro binding assays and autoradio-
grams clearly demonstrated that AD-DRK is a non-selective ligand,
binding to both Aβ and tau fibrils (Fig. 2). Significant specific binding
was detected in the temporal cortex of the APP mouse model and in the
motor cortex of brain tissues from PSP patients, which are enriched with
Aβ (SFig. 3) and tau (Fig. 3A) aggregates, respectively. Autoradiographic
examination can be significantly affected by experimental conditions,
such as sample fixation and alcohol percentage in washout solution.
Fixed sample and alcohol-rich washout solution might substantially
reduce non-specific or off-target binding of the ligand, potentially
leading to misleadingly high-performance results. 125I-DRM106 and
125I-BIP showed highly specific in-vitro binding to amyloid and tau ag-
gregates, respectively, in postmortem AD brain (Chen et al., 2015; Ono
et al., 2016). However, the use of fixed brain sections and incuba-
tion/washout solution containing 10–40% EtOH in these studies might
increase the risk of misestimating in-vivo binding characteristics. In the
present study, autoradiography was performed under natural conditions
using fresh-frozen samples and non-alcohol washout solution to better
mimic the in-vivo situation. A similar strategy was used in the develop-
ment of 123I-ABC577, a SPECT ligand already employed in clinical
studies for non-invasively detecting Aβ aggregates (Maya et al., 2016).
In contrast to the lower performance of 11C-PiB in-vitro binding, where
the AD-to-HC ratio in the temporal cortex region decreased to approx-
imately 1.2, 125I-AD-DRK demonstrated an approximately 2-fold higher
AD-to-HC ratio (Fig. 3). This improvement is likely due to the super-
imposed radio-signal from the concurrence binding to Aβ and tau de-
posits. Non-selective binding characteristic is beneficial for detecting
capacity and expands the clinical application scope by covering a wider
range of neuropathological changes in diverse neurodegenerative
disorders.

isoforms have three microtubule-binding repeats (3R), and three iso-
forms have four repeats (4R) (Goedert, 1989). The distinct conforma-
tions of tau are likely to determine subcellular, cellular, and regional
localization of tau deposits in a disease-specific fashion, provoking
characteristic symptoms associated with deteriorations of affected
