Let's talk about Sarah. She's your top performer, consistently crushing targets and driving
innovation. She also logged off at 2 p.m. yesterday to go walk her dog. And you're secretly
terrified to say anything about it.

Welcome to the era of the "Lazy Overachiever," where your most valuable employees are
working less while delivering more. And it's breaking every management principle you've
built your company on.

Remember when we all pretended to work nine hours a day? Those TPS reports didn't file
themselves, and Karen from accounting would absolutely notice if you didn't look sufficiently
stressed during your 3 p.m. coffee run. Extra bathroom breaks, coffee chats and hoping for
meetings to kill time. Owners’ nightmare now that I look back.

But here's the thing:

MORE FOR YOU

Your top performers have figured out a secret that's hiding in plain
sight.

They've realized that in a knowledge economy, productivity isn't about time spent; it's about
problems solved. And they're solving them faster than ever, leaving us traditional "time
equals dedication" believers in an existential crisis.

I talked recently with a founder of a tech startup who mentioned panicking when he realized
his best developer was only online four hours a day. But when he checked the developer's
output, he saw he was outperforming everyone else by three times. For the founder—and

The panic is real.

Across industries, business owners are watching their star players deliver weekly targets by
Wednesday, automate processes that "required" 40 hours, and take random Thursdays off
while still outperforming peers. Some are even actually using their lunch breaks (the
audacity!).

Here's the uncomfortable equation: If Sarah delivers $500,000 in value working 25 hours a
week, while Jim delivers $300,000 working 50 hours, who's really the harder worker? I had
the same challenge when it came to some colleagues I had. They looked at how many hours
I spent in the business versus the actual outputs. I was able to deliver the same outputs as
they did in one day when they were working five days.

Traditional management would say, Jim, look at those hours! He's first in, last out. He sends
emails at 10 p.m.! But modern business owners are facing a new reality:

We've been measuring the wrong thing all along.

Remember when staying late at the office was a badge of honor? Remember when seeing
the janitors meant that you were dedicated, or looking back, was it that they were not getting
the important things done in a timely fashion?

But the Lazy Overachievers have exposed this for what it really is: a remnant of industrial-
age thinking that's about as relevant as a fax machine in a TikTok meeting.

These employees aren't actually lazy; they're efficiency obsessed.

They automate repetitive tasks while others perfect their Excel shortcuts. They build systems
while others build overtime. They focus on impact, while others focus on appearance. And
most importantly, they actually have the energy to innovate because they're not burnt out.

This creates a fascinating problem: How do you pay people when they deliver more value in
less time? Traditional hourly or salary models start to look as outdated as a BlackBerry in a
5G world.

In a conversation I had with another business leader, she noted that an employee delivered a
month's worth of work in one week and then asked for a raise while simultaneously
requesting to work fewer hours. While that might sound crazy, her output made it a
reasonable request.

Here's what keeps us up at night:

We're worried about managing hybrid teams when some roles truly need fixed hours. We're
concerned about maintaining company culture with asymmetric schedules. We're anxious
about measuring productivity when hours become irrelevant. And if we're really honest, we're
worried about justifying our own long hours (ouch, this one hurts).

The rise of the Lazy Overachiever isn't a trend—it's the leading indicator of a fundamental
shift in how work works. The genie is out of the bottle, and it's wearing yoga pants while
closing million-dollar deals from a beach somewhere.

Here's what smart business owners are doing to adapt to this new
reality:

1. Stop tracking hours (unless legally required) and start tracking outcomes.

2. Rethink compensation models to reward efficiency, not presence.

3. Get comfortable with asymmetric schedules.

4. Accept that your best people might work differently than you do.

The ultimate irony? These "lazy" employees often create more value because they have the
energy and creativity that come from not being exhausted. They're not working less; they're
working smarter, and it's time we caught up.

So the next time you see your top performer logging off early, remember: The only crisis here
is in our outdated understanding of what work should look like. The Lazy Overachievers
aren't the problem; they're the future.

And they're probably getting more done than the rest of us while we're still stuck in meetings
about meetings.
