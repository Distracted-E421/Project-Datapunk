Significance
 Cultural practices can shape the 
transmission of learned 
behaviors by affecting who learns 
from whom. If cultural traditions 
cause people to tend to learn 
languages from mothers or 
maternal relatives, this biased 
cultural transmission could 
create a closer-than-expected 
association between language 
variation and maternally 
inherited genetic variation. 
We use genetic, linguistic, and 
ethnographic information from 
populations across the world to 
find evidence of sex-biased 
transmission at both global and 
local scales. We account for the 
relationships between genes, 
languages, and geography to find 
that language transmission may 
have a local-level sex bias in 
certain regions of the world, and 
that the effects of ethnographic 
traits, such as descent and 
postmarital residence, are 
inconsistent at a global level.

ANTHROPOLOGY
Genetic and linguistic comparisons reveal complex sex-­biased 
transmission of language features
Yakov Pichkara,b
, Alexandra Surowieca,c, and Nicole Creanzaa,b,1
Affiliations are included on p. 9.
Edited by Anne Kandler, Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology, Leipzig, Germany; received February 20, 2024; accepted August 26, 2024 by 
Editorial Board Member Marcus W. Feldman
The history of people’s movements and interactions shapes both genetic and linguistic 
variation. Genes and languages are transmitted separately and their distributions reflect 
different aspects of human history, but some demographic processes can cause them to 
be similarly distributed. In particular, forms of societal organization, including move-
ments in and out of a community, may have shaped the transmission of both genes and 
languages. If children were more likely to learn their mother’s language than their father’s 
when their parents were from populations that spoke different languages or dialects, 
then language variation might show a closer association with maternally transmitted 
genetic markers than autosomal ones; this association could be further reinforced if 
children reside with predominantly maternal kin. We analyze the worldwide relationship 
between linguistic and genomic variation, leveraging the sex-­biased transmission of X 
chromosomes to assess whether language has tended to be preferentially transmitted 
along the male or female line. In addition, we measure the effects of postmarital residence 
with female kin, matrilineal descent, and endogamy on the covariation of mitochondrial 
DNA and languages, using mtDNA because genomic data were available for very few 
populations with these ethnographic traits. We find that while there is little evidence 
for a consistent or widespread sex bias in the transmission of language, such biased 
transmission may have occurred locally in several parts of the world and might have 
been influenced by population-­level ethnographic characteristics, such as female-­based 
descent or residence patterns. Our results highlight the complex relationships between 
genes, language, ethnography, and geography.

 An individual’s ancestry can refer to many forms of inheritance, encompassing the history 
of one’s genes, beliefs, languages, traditions, and material cultures. Language can serve as 
a distinct marker of identity that can be learned either from family, community, or both 
( 1 ). Since roughly 1,100 AD, there is documentation from multiple European languages 
that a person’s native language was often called a “mother tongue” (e.g. Muttersprache , 
﻿moedertaal , modersmål , lingua materna , etc.), a phrasing that suggests maternally biased 
transmission of a vernacular language ( 2 ), perhaps used at first in a pejorative sense as a 
contrast to Latin as the more refined sermo patrius (“language of the fathers”) ( 3 ). When 
communities are relatively isolated from one another, genes and languages are generally 
inherited from the same sources, resulting in parallel distributions of genetic and linguistic 
diversity ( 4 ,  5 ). Different features of languages—such as sounds, words, and syntax—can 
potentially be influenced by different cultural sources ( 6 ), which could cause some of these 
features to align better with genetic ancestry than others ( 6     – 9 ). Still, the idea of a “mother 
tongue” prompts the question of whether the phrase is purely metaphorical, or if further 
exploration of the observed associations between language and genetics ( 5 ,  10 ,  11 ) might 
show signatures of sex-biased processes. If, across human history, children were more likely 
to speak their mother’s language than their father’s when the two differed, then we expect 
to find an association between genetics and language that is stronger for genetic markers 
that are inherited maternally.
 Groups of people around the world have many differing kinship structures and residence 
patterns, and the histories of these cultural traits vary by region ( 12 ). For example, in 
societies with matrilineal descent systems, descent group membership is traced along the 
maternal line; in matrilocal postmarital residence systems, a couple primarily resides with 
the wife’s kin. In contrast, patrilineal or patrilocal systems include descent traced along 
the paternal line and postmarital residence with the husband’s kin. In communities where 
these descent or residence structures have been consistent, there may be predictable pat-
terns of cultural transmission over time ( 13 ). There are many other forms of descent and

residence patterns, but these female- and male-based patterns 
provide a route for sex-biased cultural transmission: For example, 
if a couple’s children are surrounded by their father’s kin in their 
early years, they are likely to learn language features and cultural 
knowledge from their father’s family. A patrilocal or patrilineal 
society can retain cultural traditions during female-biased admix-
ture from another population, in spite of the genetic mixing 
between the two populations. In regions where patrilocality is 
common, the genetic variation of nonrecombining Y haplogroups 
tends to track linguistic divisions, such as between languages  
( 14 ,  15 ) or language families ( 15 ,  16 ). For example, the Munda 
are Austroasiatic speakers in India who are geographically distant 
from other Austroasiatic-speaking populations that reside mostly 
in southeast Asia ( 17 ). Due to their historically patrilocal kinship, 
the Y haplogroups of Munda individuals are more similar to those 
of other Austroasiatic speakers, whereas the rest of their genome 
shows more admixture with other populations in India ( 17 ).
 Similarly, matrilocality can have strong effects on maternally 
inherited mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). Among Austronesian- 
speaking populations, a history of matrilocality has led to less 
admixture of mtDNA haplogroups than autosomal DNA and Y 
haplogroups ( 18 – 20 ). Sex-biased admixture can also be brought 
about by other factors, such as the enslavement of Africans in the 
Americas ( 21 ). The descendants of these slaves have more matrilin-
eal African ancestry and patrilineal European ancestry than expected 
based on the frequency of these groups in the population, and being 
forced to speak European languages also disconnected these slaves 
and their descendants from the linguistic history of their ancestors 
( 21 ). In all of these cases, the effects of significant demographic 
processes can be observed in both language and genetics.
 These examples of sex-biased gene-language dynamics take 
place at the scale of communities, but large-scale analyses of genes 
and languages point to the role of anthropological traits in shaping 
human diversity. At a worldwide scale, language features have been 
found to covary with genetics ( 4 ,  5 ), and there is some evidence 
of male-biased language transmission, with nonrecombining Y 
chromosome variation correlating with languages ( 22 ). It is pos-
sible that this covariation of language and genes may have occurred 
due to sex-dependent processes, and if it has, then it can be inves-
tigated through analyses of variation of the sex chromosomes and 
mtDNA. Some groups that have a long history of patriliny, such 
as Bantu-speaking populations in Central and Southern Africa 
( 23 ,  24 ), continued speaking Bantu languages even after local 
women who speak non-Bantu languages were integrated into the 
community ( 25 ). Similarly, the spread of farming in North 
America may have coincided with the expansion of the speakers 
of Uto-Aztecan languages, causing the languages of this region to 
correlate with certain Y haplogroups ( 26 ). In patrilocal groups in 
Indonesia, Y chromosomes were more similar between groups 
speaking the same language than groups speaking different lan-
guages, suggesting that the practice of patrilocality corresponded 
to language being transmitted along male lines ( 27 ). In contrast, 
the differences between the languages spoken in parts of South 
America seem to be associated more closely with mitochondrial 
than autosomal variation, and in some of these regions, females 
were predicted to primarily migrate between populations within 
the same linguistic group ( 28 ). Finally, among Indo-European lan-
guages, researchers found a mixed relationship in which the sounds 
of speech are correlated with mtDNA variation whereas language 
lexicons are correlated with Y-chromosomal variation ( 29 ).
 In addition to postmarital residence patterns, community mar-
riage organization can have an effect on both genetic and cultural 
transmission. In endogamous populations, marriages typically 
occur between members of the same community, and in exogamous 
populations, the norm is to marry outside of the community; aga-
mous populations do not follow either pattern. These population- 
level patterns can influence variation in both genes and languages: 
Endogamy can make the histories of genes and languages more 
similar ( 30 ), whereas exogamy can cause them to separate ( 31 ). In 
genetic studies in India, endogamous groups were found to have 
high within-population genetic similarity ( 32 ); further, when 
endogamous groups are genetically isolated for a long period of 
time, this stratification can result in genetic differentiation between 
groups, as in Indian castes ( 33 ). In contrast, a population with high 
diversity of mtDNA haplotypes is consistent with a pattern of 
patrilocal exogamy, in which males in one population marry 
women from other populations and reside with the males’ kin 
( 34 – 36 ), and high mtDNA diversity was also observed in the 
northwest Amazon among populations that practice linguistic 
exogamy, in which marriage partners are found from other popu-
lations in different language groups ( 37 ). In Central Asia, geograph-
ical exogamy—where marriage partners are chosen from large 
distances—may counterintuitively decrease genetic diversity, espe-
cially when the method of choosing a partner is tied closely to 
kinship ( 38 ).
 These studies express how different human social histories 
impact the relationship between genes and learned behaviors. In 
addition to focusing on particular regions or communities, we 
suggest that a global analysis can help determine whether there 
are any universal patterns that suggest a sex-biased relationship 
between genes and language. Although uniparental mtDNA and 
Y-DNA contribute valuable information about specific popula-
tions or regions, large-scale analyses with multiple distantly related 
populations benefit from whole-genome sequences ( 39 ). To date, 
no study has used genomic data to analyze the relationship 
between languages and genetics around the world; prior studies 
have focused on particular regions or used microsatellite data, 
which limits the scope of linguistic and genetic comparisons.
 Here, we analyze phonemes, the smallest units of sound capable 
of distinguishing meaning between words, which can be compared 
both within and between language families to facilitate worldwide 
analyses. We then match genotyped populations to the phoneme 
inventories of their spoken language. To better understand how 
sex-biased admixture and gene flow affect language diversity, we 
jointly analyze genetic and linguistic data in two ways. First, we 
measure genome-wide variation from both autosomes and X chro-
mosomes to ask whether the maternal bias in the inheritance of 
X chromosomes can be used to detect a maternal bias in language 
transmission. Next, we compile mitochondrial genomes, which 
have available data for multiple populations with matrilineal 
descent or matrilocal residence patterns; we hypothesize that these 
ethnographic traits might further amplify patterns of maternally 
biased language transmission. By examining variation in multiple 
types of genetic data in conjunction with phonemic and ethno-
graphic data, we aim to shed light on worldwide patterns of lin-
guistic and genomic variation, particularly in the context of 
possible sex-biased cultural transmission.

 We then modified our model to use mitochondrial data, which 
was available for 159 populations that could be matched to a 
spoken language with available phoneme data and enabled us to 
more uniformly incorporate ethnographic data; our set of popu-
lations included 146 populations with data on descent (16 mat-
rilineal and 130 nonmatrilineal), 150 populations with data on 
postmarital residence (28 female-based and 122 non-female-based 
postmarital residence), and 127 populations with data on com-
munity marriage organization (36 exogamous, 50 agamous, 41 
endogamous). We used this model to measure how these descent 
and residence patterns affected the relationship between language, 
genes, and genetics. When analyzing all populations together, we 
found that mtDNA and spatial distances were positively correlated 
with linguistic distances ( Fig. 2 B and C , solid lines), and that the 
effects of the ethnographic traits were mixed. On this worldwide 
scale, societies that reside with female kin after marriage have a 
more negative relationship between mtDNA and language, and a 
slightly negative relationship between spatial and linguistic simi-
larity compared to societies that do not reside with female kin 
( Fig. 2B ). In contrast, for populations with matrilineal descent, 
mtDNA was positively associated with linguistic variation at short 
distances compared to populations with nonmatrilineal descent 
( Fig. 2C  , dashed red line), suggesting that matrilineal descent sys-
tems might influence language transmission at the local level. At 
larger distance thresholds, mtDNA does not appear to give addi-
tional information about language in matrilineal societies com-
pared to nonmatrilineal ones. Finally, endogamous populations 
showed a consistently positive geography-language association and 
tended to have a neutral or slightly negative association between 
genes and language ( Fig. 2D ). As with the previous analysis, the 
posterior distributions that are furthest from zero correspond to 
those coefficients with the lowest P﻿-values in a non-Bayesian 
model (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 ).

Discussion
 Here, we analyze the relationship between languages and genes to 
measure the distribution of sex-biased language transmission 
around the world. Similar to other studies, we find that in much 
of the world, autosomal genetic similarity is positively correlated 
with language similarity after controlling for geographic distances 
( 5 ). At short spatial distances, we found that autosomal variation 
was even more closely correlated to language than geography was 
( Fig. 1C ). We found this close gene-language relationship when 
considering mtDNA distances as well ( Fig. 2 B and C ). When 
controlling for both autosomal and geographic variation, however, 
we find that sex-biased X chromosome genetics only help explain 
language variation at very large distances of more than 7,500 km.
 If languages or their characteristics are more likely to be trans-
mitted by mothers than by fathers over many generations, there 
should be a closer relationship between language and the part of 
genetic variation that is female-biased, i.e., mtDNA and X chro-
mosome variation ( 18 – 20 ). Due to the differing inheritance pat-
terns of autosomes and X chromosomes and the different effective 
population sizes of the two, we do not expect identical patterns 
of interpopulation distances ( 40 ). Models of migration that take 
these different patterns of inheritance into account suggest that 
the disproportionate movement of males, in particular, can cause 
the FST of autosomal and of X-linked loci to deviate from one 
another ( 41 ), a pattern that we find in our data, with the two 
measurements highly correlated but with a positive slope such that 
X chromosome FST exceeds autosomal FST more at higher FST 
values (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 ). Previous researchers have used this 
difference between autosomal and X chromosome FST to under-
stand how migration and population size have influenced the 
relationships between populations ( 42 ,  43 ).
 By accounting for the relationship between autosomes and X 
chromosomes ( 41 ), we do not find that the covariation of genes 
and phonemes provides evidence for a primarily matrilineal trans-
mission of language worldwide, but we find that there are several 
regions in which it is plausible. In particular, we find that Africa 
has a higher concentration of populations with a positive relation-
ship between X chromosome and language similarity ( Fig. 1 ), and 
also between mtDNA and language similarity ( Fig. 2 ). In particular,

chromosome variation and language ( Fig. 1B  ). The effects of these 
ethnographic traits are not consistent around the world, however. 
In Southeast Asia and the Pacific, where there are also many mat-
rilocal populations, we find that a pattern of residence with female 
kin negatively affected the relationship between mtDNA and lan-
guage ( Fig. 3 C  and D  ), contrary to previous work on matrilocality 
and language transmission in this region ( 18 – 20 ). In the Americas, 
we also find that the ethnographic traits we tested did not affect the 
gene-language relationship (SI Appendix, Figs. S3 and S4 ).
 Surprisingly, our worldwide analysis suggests that matrilineal 
descent, rather than marital residence with female kin, contributes 
to a positive relationship between mtDNA and language. Since 
language is learned not only from parents, but also communities 
( 45 ), we expected residence patterns to affect the relationship 
between genes, geography, and language more strongly than the 
mode of descent. Instead, when we analyze populations worldwide, 
we find that female-biased residence patterns decrease the similarity 
between mtDNA and language variation at a threshold less than 
1,500 km ( Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 ), and that matrilineal 
descent increases it ( Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 ). In Africa, 
however, both matrilineal descent and postmarital residence with 
female kin appear to strengthen the gene-language association at 
intermediate spatial distances less than ~5,000 km ( Fig. 3 A and 
﻿B ). This may be due to the interaction between historically matri-
local or ambilocal Khoe-San communities such as the Nama ( 46 ) 
and the relatively recent expansion of Bantu-speaking peoples into 
southern Africa ( 25 ). Since we use phoneme repertoires to calculate 
linguistic distances, this pattern could also be explained by a history 
of sex-biased admixture in the region: After Bantu-speaking peoples 
entered southern Africa, research suggests that Bantu men had chil-
dren with women from the Khoe-San populations already living 
there, which introduced both female-biased gene flow and linguistic 
features such as click  consonants to Bantu-speaking populations 
( 47 ,  48 ).
 While there is some evidence of historical matrilineal descent 
among Bantu-speaking and Austronesian societies ( 49 ), their his-
tories also suggest that switching occurred often between matrilin-
eally biased, patrilineally biased, and neutral systems ( 50 ). If this 
switching is truly common, then even if residence patterns have a 
strong effect on language transmission, the potential channels for 
language transmission may not remain stable long enough to pro-
duce large-scale sex-biased patterns ( 27 ). In addition, the spread of 
agriculture and pastoralism may have led to a change in residence 
and descent patterns, and interfered with the effects of possibly 
widespread matrilineal descent ( 23 ,  51 ). As a result, in most of the 
world, sex-biased gene-language patterns that had once existed 
could have since been obscured. This agrees with our finding that 
anthropological traits and the relationship between language and 
the X chromosome have no consistent global pattern, and appeared 
to depend instead on local dynamics in recent history.
 We also contrast these analyses of ethnographic traits with 
sex-biased patterns in how societies organize marriages and choose 
marriage partners, in particular whether there is a cultural tradition 
of partnering with a member of the same community (endogamy) 
or a different community. Even though isolation due to endogamy 
could cause genes and languages to covary ( 30 ), we find that endog-
amous populations around the world do not appear to have a 
greater gene-language correlation than nonendogamous popula-
tions at any scale of analysis ( Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 ).

Both in our worldwide analysis and the regional analyses of Central 
and East Asia and Western Eurasia, the geographic distances 
between endogamous populations and their neighbors tend to 
positively predict their language variation, although we do not find 
this in other regional analyses ( Figs. 2D and  3 and SI Appendix,

any gene-language covariation that occurs among endogamous

relationships between genes, geography, and language do not con-
sistently differ for endogamous populations, and individual pop-
ulations can vary greatly in these relationships.
 There are several ways in which our worldwide study of genes 
and language can be extended. We focused on interpopulation 
analyses, both because the phoneme data were available on a 
language level and because many of the genotyped populations 
had only a handful of sequenced individuals. With more densely 
sampled language and genetic data, it would be possible to 
investigate finer-scale patterns that could help us understand 
the within-population processes of language transmission and 
local movement [e.g. ( 11 )]. In addition, previous work has used 
Y chromosome variation to better understand the history of 
populations and to disentangle the effects of ethnographic traits, 
particularly through comparisons with mtDNA variation 
( 18 ,  19 ,  24 ). We attempted to do this type of comparison as 
well, but we were limited by the availability of Y chromosome 
SNP data; we could not find adequate Y-DNA data for most 
populations with sufficient mtDNA sequences. Similarly, our 
sample of nuclear genetic data includes only three populations 
that were historically matrilineal or practiced female-kin marital 
residence, and included 40 societies for which we lacked eth-
nographic data. Due to this sampling and lack of information, 
we could only measure the impact of ethnographic traits on the 
gene-language relationship for mitochondrial genetic data and 
not for nuclear genetic data. In addition, the differences between 
transmission of phonology, lexicon, and syntax mean that these 
other linguistic elements may have patterns of covariation with 
genetics that we cannot observe through an analysis of pho-
nemes alone ( 6 ).
 In this study of language transmission, we test for wide-reaching, 
worldwide effects of ethnographic traits that may have consistently 
influenced the gene-language relationship at smaller scales ( 13 ,  17 ), 
particularly those that may have affected language transmission in 
a sex-dependent manner. Many other cultural, societal, and envi-
ronmental traits may affect the transmission of both genetic and 
linguistic features ( 45 ). The transmission of individual language 
features varies based on their importance and their use in a society, 
such that more commonly used phrases, words, and grammatical 
features will be more likely to be transmitted to the next generation 
( 9 ). Environmental factors such as humidity influence vocal cords 
and changes from one environment to another make the develop-
ment of new phonology more likely ( 52 ,  53 ). In addition to these 
linguistic and environmental variables, social factors such as popu-
lation size can affect the transmission of linguistic features ( 54 ). 
Languages with more speakers tend to have larger phoneme inven-
tories ( 55 ,  56 ) and simpler grammars ( 57 ,  58 ), although these find-
ings have been contested ( 59 ). If these social and environmental 
factors influence gene flow or other processes in population genet-
ics—e.g. if a humid rainforest isolates some populations—they 
could lead to correlations between genes and language.
 It should also be noted that the cultural categories in the 
Ethnographic Atlas tend not to completely describe the customs 
of an entire community, and the trait identified in modern pop-
ulations may not have been present in an ancestral population. 
Kinship systems change over time, sometimes as a consequence 
of the costs of maintaining that system ( 60 ) or due to other cul-
tural traits influencing its evolution ( 51 ). Nevertheless, kinship 
systems are among the more stable cultural traits ( 49 ), and their 
stability in some groups of people for over thousands of years has 
had clear genetic consequences ( 17 ). These patterns suggest that 
traits such as descent and residence patterns are likely to have 
affected the transmission of both genetics and language, making 
them ideal candidates for analysis.
 Previous studies have found that ethnographic patterns can shape 
the relationship between language and genetics. We conducted a 
set of analyses to measure these effects on a worldwide scale, and 
we find that the effects of residence or kinship patterns on genes 
and languages are not generally consistent across the globe and that 
population endogamy did not measurably affect the gene-language 
relationship. Overall, we find that maternally biased X chromosome 
genetics tend not to be predictive of local language variation, and 
those regions where they have a positive predictive effect are not 
historically matrilocal or matrilineal. However, for mtDNA, we 
observe that matrilineal descent increases the positive association 
between linguistic and mitochondrial genetic variation at relatively 
short distances (less than ~2,000 km), which appears to be primarily 
driven by populations in Africa, particularly those in the matrilineal 
belt ( 44 ). In contrast to the worldwide pattern, we observe that both 
matrilineal descent and residence with female kin in Africa increase 
the association between linguistic and mitochondrial genetic vari-
ation across relatively large geographic distances. These results sug-
gest that Africa could be an important focus of future genetic studies 
that consider language transmission and cultural traditions in more 
detail, especially those that include Y chromosome data and numer-
ous individuals per population. 
Methods
Genomic Data Collection and Processing. For nuclear genomic data, we used 
three sources: the 1,000 Genomes Project, the Human Genome Diversity Project, 
and the Simons Genome Diversity Project (61–63) (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 for a 
flowchart of the data processing). We used PLINK 2.0 to find biallelic SNPs with 
call rates of at least 1% and with a Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium exact test P-­value 
less than 10−7 (using the midp and keep-­fewhet options) and only kept those 
individuals with sex identified in the associated metadata (64). Next, we pruned 
SNPs closely linked to one another (with a linkage disequilibrium r2 threshold 
of 0.2 and a window size of 500 kilobases). After these filters, we were left with 
1,724,738 variants on the autosomes and 34,257 on the X chromosome and 
3,102 individuals from 159 populations. We then removed 22 individuals who 
were the only members of their populations, leaving 3,080 individuals from 137 
populations. Genetic distances were calculated with PLINK2 to find the FST for 
both autosomes and X chromosomes using the method described in Bhatia et al. 
(65) based on the Hudson FST estimator (66). To estimate FST for X chromosomes, 
PLINK2 accounts for the difference in sample size presented by having a mix of 
males and females by calculating the number of samples, multiplying by two, 
and subtracting the number of males.
For our subsequent language analyses, we began with a presence–absence 
matrix of phonemes [describing the presence (1) or absence (0) of 728 phonemes 
in 2,082 languages] used by Creanza et al. (5), which was originally compiled 
from primary sources by Merritt Ruhlen. We attempted to match each genotyped 
population to a language in the database of phoneme inventories used in (5). 
We first searched for exact matches by comparing the population name with 
language names and verified the match with the provided sampling locations. 
If no exact match was found, we searched for alternate spellings and alternate 
names for both the population and language/dialects in the region. We then 
removed populations with no phonemic data, which left 3,062 individuals from 
130 populations (Dataset S1).
We attempted to match each genotyped population to ethnographic traits 
using Murdock’s Ethnographic Atlas (44), which has been digitized in the D-­PLACE 
database (67). We used both the population and language fields in D-­PLACE to

Mitochondrial Data Collection and Processing. Thus, in addition to our 
nuclear genomic data, we searched the literature and the NCBI database to find 
sequenced mitochondrial genomes that included population information for the 
genotyped individuals (SI Appendix, Fig. S15 for a flowchart of the mitochondrial 
data processing). In total, we collected complete mitochondrial DNA from 4,878 
individuals from 159 populations (each of which had at least three individuals, 
and up to 210; SI Appendix, Fig. S12). For each population, we found the average 
mtDNA distance to every other population using Kimura’s 2-­parameter model with 
the R package, ape, as we did not have enough samples from many populations 
to use FST (68). Alternate distance metrics based on different models of evolution 
(e.g. JC69, K81, TN93) produced very similar results: These other models return 
metrics nearly identical to those produced with Kimura’s 2-­parameter model, with 
coefficients of determination (R2) of 1.0000 and median residuals between the 
2-­parameter model and the three other models equal to 3.852 × 10–7, 1.146 × 
10–8, and –3.970 × 10–8 respectively, which is very small compared to the mean 
distance with these models, 2.0 × 10–3. As such, we chose the default 2-­parameter 
model. Accession numbers and D-­PLACE population matches for mitochondrial 
sequences are available on GitHub (github.com/CreanzaLab/MothersOrOthers) 
and Figshare (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.27058444.v1) (69) and as 
Datasets S2 and S3.
Since mtDNA is available for more populations than for those with SNP data, we 
could collect mtDNA for many populations with ethnographic traits categorized in the 
D-­PLACE database (67). We searched for mtDNA for populations with ethnographic 
data in D-­PLACE by including each population name in a search for mitochondrial 
genomes on NCBI and Google Scholar. We also searched for “PopSets” of complete 
mitochondrial genomes on NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/popset), which pro-
vide sequences for a set of populations from a single study. For these sequences, we 
attempted to match each population with mtDNA to a society in D-­PLACE, using both 
the population and language fields in D-­PLACE to assign genotyped populations to 
ethnographic records. We classified 150 populations with a postmarital residence pat-
tern (biased toward female kin (e.g. matrilocal, avunculocal, or uxorilocal) or not (e.g. 
patrilocal, virilocal, ambilocal); Ethnographic Atlas code EA012), and 145 populations 
with a primary pattern of descent (biased toward female kin or not; Ethnographic 
Atlas code EA043) (Table 2), and 127 populations with data on community marriage 
organization (36 exogamous, 50 agamous, 41 endogamous; Ethnographic Atlas 
code EA015). For two sets of sequenced populations, the paper associated with the 
NCBI GenBank entry provided information on postmarital residence, so we included 
this information in our database (70, 71). Overall, we linked 159 populations with 
phoneme and ethnographic data to mitogenome data.
Constructing a Geographic Distance Matrix. Each of the populations in the 
Ruhlen phoneme database was associated with a population in the Ethnologue 
database by Creanza et al. (5). For populations with more than one potential 
language match in the Ruhlen database (as noted in Dataset S1), if only one 
language had phoneme data, we used that data; if more than one of these 
languages had data in the Ruhlen phoneme database, we used the average 
phoneme presence across all languages for which we have record of enduring 
usage (e.g. if three languages are spoken by a population and two use a particular 
phoneme, we represent the value of that phoneme in the population as two-­
thirds). The Ethnologue, a global language database, contains language family 
information and identifies a geographic location corresponding roughly to the 
centroid of the area where each language is spoken, given in latitude-­longitude 
coordinates (72). For the spatial distance matrix, we used these coordinates to cal-
culate the geographic distance between each pair of languages. Following other 
studies that consider patterns of isolation-­by-­distance and human migration on 
a worldwide scale (5, 73), we use great-­circle distance between populations on 
a linear scale; when languages were in different continental regions, we used 
waypoints to approximate the distance of human migration across land masses 
(5, 73). These waypoints were located in Anadyr, Russia (64 N, 177E); Cairo, Egypt 
(30 N, 31E); Istanbul, Türkiye (41 N, 28E); Phnom Penh, Cambodia (11 N, 104E); 
and Prince Rupert, Canada (54 N, 130 W). The distance between two points is 
then the sum of the great circle distances between the points and the waypoint(s) 
between them. The locations in Ethnologue tended to be near the locations where 
individuals’ genetic data was sampled (median distance <200 km), but there 
were disparities between these two locations for a few diasporic communities 
(SI Appendix, Fig. S13), so we conducted analyses with the Ethnologue locations.
Gene-­language Analysis. For our nuclear genomic analysis, to compare the 
effect of geography, autosomal variation, and X chromosome variation on lin-
guistic similarity, we first accounted for the covariation among the factors. Since 
human demographic history leaves a signature on genetic variation, such that 
genetic distance is generally correlated to geographic distance, we first controlled 
for the effect of geography on autosomal genetic distance by finding the linear 
relationship between spatial and autosomal distances. We used residuals of this 
linear model, i.e. the genetic distances between populations after accounting for 
spatial distance, as our autosomal distances. Similarly, for the X chromosome, we 
used a linear model to find X chromosome distance between populations that was 
uncorrelated with both spatial and autosomal distances. After these data trans-
formations, we had spatial distances between populations, autosomal genetic 
distances (after accounting for spatial distances), and X chromosome distances 
(after accounting for spatial and autosomal distances). Before using these in our 
models, we normalized each set of distances to have a SD of 1.
We used a binomial model to describe the relationship between the number 
of shared phonemes between populations and interpopulation distances using 
the rethinking package (74). We found the number of phonemes that are found 
in one or both populations (total phonemes) as well as phonemes that were pres-
ent in both populations (shared phonemes). We then fit a binomial distribution 
to model shared phonemes as a function of spatial (S), autosomal (GA), and X 
chromosome (GX) distances:
shared phonemes ∼Binomial(total phonemes, 1 −p)
logit(p) = S + GA + GX + e
The priors for all coefficients (for S, GA, and GX) and for the intercept e were nor-
mally distributed, with a variance of 1 for the coefficients and 3 for the intercept. 
In this Bayesian analysis, we analyzed the posterior distributions of the coeffi-
cients for spatial and genetic effects. Where the posterior distribution is more 
separated from 0, we infer that there is stronger evidence that the variable has 
a nonzero effect on linguistic similarity. In addition, we fit this binomial model

­independently for each population that had six or more populations within

linguistic similarity to other populations within 2,500 km.
For our mitochondrial DNA analysis, the binomial model was similar, except 
that instead of having two forms of genetic data, we had one. In addition, in this 
model, we tested whether ethnographic traits (matrilineal descent, female-­based 
kin-­residence, or endogamy) interacted with the effect of genes and geography 
on language. After controlling for the effect of spatial distance S on mtDNA dis-
tances Gmt and including an error term e, we ran the model with one coefficient 
if either population was matrilocal (or matrilineal, or endogamous), and another 
if they were not:
shared phonemes ∼Binomial(total phonemes, 1 −p)
logit(p) = S[ethn] + Gmt[ethn] + e
The difference between the coefficient’s posteriors is then the effect of the 
thnographic trait (ethn) on the relationship between language, geography, and 
genes (e.g. the effect of matrilineality on the spatial coefficient is S[matrilineal] 
– S[nonmatrilineal]). Similarly to the model of autosomal variation, we fit this 
binomial model using a non-­Bayesian generalized linear model to measure the 
P-­values for each coefficient. In addition, we reran this model after subsetting 
the data into broad regions—Africa, the Americas, East Asia and the Pacific, and 
West Eurasia (SI Appendix, Fig.  S10)—such that at each geographic distance 
threshold we only compared pairs of populations if at least one population was 
in that subset. Similarly to our model with autosomal and X chromosome data, 
we plotted the coefficient of mtDNA (Gmt) on language on a map at a thresh-
old of 2,500 km. The code for these analyses is available on GitHub (github.
