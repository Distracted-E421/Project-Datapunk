Background:  Prior  efforts  have  revealed  changes  in  gut  microbiome,  circulating  metabolome, and  multimodal 
neuroimaging  features  in  cerebral  small  vessel  disease  (CSVD).  However,  there  is  a  paucity  of  research  inte-
grating the multi-omic information to characterize the role of the microbiota-gut-brain axis in CSVD.
Methods: We collected gut microbiome, fecal and blood metabolome, multimodal magnetic resonance imaging 
data from 37 CSVD patients with white matter hyperintensities and 46 healthy controls. Between-group com-
parison was performed to identify the differential gut microbial taxa, followed by performance of multi-stage 
microbiome-metabolome-neuroimaging-neuropsychology correlation analyses in CSVD patients.
Results:  Our  data  showed  both  depleted  and  enriched  gut  microbes  in  CSVD  patients.  Among  the  differential 
microbes, Haemophilus and Akkermansia were associated with a range of metabolites enriched for Aminoacyl- 
tRNA biosynthesis pathway. Furthermore, the affected metabolites were associated with neuroimaging measures 
involving  gray  matter  morphology,  spontaneous  intrinsic  brain  activity,  white  matter  integrity,  and  global 
structural network topology, which were in turn related to cognition and emotion in CSVD patients.
Conclusion:  Our  findings  provide  an  integrative  framework  to  understand  the  pathophysiological  mechanisms 
underlying the interplay between gut microbiota dysbiosis and CSVD, highlighting the potential of targeting the 
microbiota-gut-brain axis as a therapeutic strategy in CSVD patients.

1. Introduction

Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) describes a syndrome of clin-
ical,  cognitive,  neuroimaging  and  neuropathological  findings  that are 
thought to arise from disease affecting the perforating cerebral arteri-
oles, capillaries and venules (Wardlaw et al., 2013a). Features of CSVD 
on  magnetic  resonance  imaging  (MRI)  include  white  matter  hyper-
intensities  (WMH),  recent  small  subcortical  infarcts,  lacunes,  peri-
vascular  spaces,  microbleeds,  and  brain  atrophy  (Wardlaw  et  al., 
2013b). Among these imaging biomarkers, WMH, resulting from chronic 
ischaemia associated with CSVD, are the most common and often the 
earliest  brain  tissue  changes  (Joutel  and  Chabriat,  2017).  Existing 
research  has  shown  that  WMH  are  important  for  clinical  outcome  in 

terms of cognitive and functional impairment, and have a crucial role in 
the aetiology of dementia (Georgakis et al., 2019; Prins and Scheltens, 
2015). Moreover, interventions targeting WMH can be categorized as 
primary  and 
symptomatic  and 
disease-modifying treatment of vascular dementia (Prins and Scheltens, 
2015).

Although several possible mechanisms of communication between the 
gut microbiota and the brain have been identified (Cryan et al., 2019), 
there  has  been  growing  interest  in  microbial  metabolic  pathways 
(McCarville et al., 2020), as the relationships of the gut microbiota with 
fecal and circulating metabolites have been well established (Visconti 
et  al.,  2019;  Vojinovic  et  al.,  2019;  Zierer  et  al.,  2018).  Given  their 
importance in host-microbiota crosstalk, research focusing on gut mi-
crobial  metabolites  has  attracted  intense  interest  from  investigators 
(Krautkramer et al., 2021; McCarville et al., 2020).

There have been prior attempts to reveal changes in gut microbiome, 
circulating  metabolome,  and  multimodal  neuroimaging  features  in 
CSVD.  Utilizing  gut  microbiome  profiling,  Cai  et  al.  found  that  gut 
microbiota composition of arteriosclerotic CSVD patients differed from 
that of healthy controls (HC), and the altered microbiota composition 
was associated with WMH (Cai et al., 2021). Using blood metabolomic 
profiling,  earlier  studies  have  demonstrated  that  multiple  distinct 
metabolic  signatures  are  associated  with  CSVD  imaging  markers 
(including  WMH),  stroke,  cognition,  and  dementia  (Harshfield  and 
Markus,  2023;  Harshfield  et  al.,  2022).  Recent  work  also  shows  that 
circulating metabolomic measures, including multiple lipid and nonlipid 
metabolites,  are  associated  with  WMH  in  a  general  population  of 
middle-aged and older adults (Sliz et al., 2022). In addition, mounting 
evidence from multimodal neuroimaging points to abnormalities in gray 
matter morphology (Kim et al., 2016; Lambert et al., 2016), spontaneous 
intrinsic  brain  activity  (Chen  et  al.,  2024;  Schulz  et  al.,  2021),  white 
matter  integrity  (Kim  et  al.,  2016;  Zeestraten  et  al.,  2017),  and 
graph-based network topology (Gesierich et al., 2020; Tuladhar et al., 
2020) in CSVD patients. Despite these previous efforts, there is a paucity 
of research integrating the multi-omic information to characterize the 
role of the microbiota-gut-brain axis in CSVD.

In  the  present  study,  we  aimed  to  investigate  the  microbiota-gut- 
brain  axis  in  CSVD  patients  with  WMH  using  a  combined  analysis  of 
gut  microbiome,  fecal  and  blood  metabolome,  and  multimodal  MRI 
data,  which  may  provide  integrated  information  on  the  underlying 
pathophysiological  mechanisms.  Building  on  the  literature,  we  hy-
pothesized  there  would  be  a  gut  microbiome-metabolome-brain- 
behavior pathway that underlies the interplay between gut microbiota 
dysbiosis and CSVD.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Participants

To  minimize  the  confounds  of  heterogeneity,  only  CSVD  patients 
with  WMH  were  recruited  from  Department  of  Neurology,  The  First 
Affiliated  Hospital  of  Anhui  Medical  University  from  August  2020  to 
July 2022, consecutively. We also enrolled HC from the local commu-
nity via poster advertisements. A total of 83 right-handed participants 
were  enrolled,  including  37  CSVD  patients  and  46  gender-  and  age- 
matched  HC.  All  patients  recruited  in  the  study  were  eligible  for  the 
following inclusion criteria: (i) age between 50 and 80 years; (ii) at least 
one risk factor for arteriosclerosis, including smoking, body mass index 
(BMI)  > 28  kg/m2,  hypertension,  diabetes,  hyperlipidemia,  coronary 
heart disease, hyperhomocysteinemia, and symptomatic stroke history; 
(iii) at least one common clinical symptom of CSVD, including cognitive 
decline,  gait,  balance  disturbance,  emotional  or  sleep  disorders,  and 
urinary and fecal disorders; (iv) WMH: periventricular Fazekas score = 3 
or  subcortical  Fazekas  score  ≥ 2  (Wahlund  et  al.,  2001).  Exclusion 
criteria  were  as  follows:  (i)  other  neuroimaging  standards  of  CSVD, 
including  small  subcortical  infarcts,  lacunes,  perivascular  spaces, 
microbleeds,  and  brain  atrophy  (Wardlaw  et  al.,  2013b);  (ii) 
non-vasculogenic  WMH  mimics  (e.g.,  multiple  sclerosis);  (iii)  other 
neurological or psychiatric disorders; (iv) brain tumors or other systemic

2.2. Neuropsychological assessment

All  participants  underwent  a  standardized  neuropsychological 
assessment  protocol  performed  by  experienced  neuropsychologists. 
General cognition was evaluated by the Montreal Cognitive Assessment 
(MoCA)  and  Mini-Mental  State  Examination  (MMSE).  Moreover,  a 
neuropsychological battery that included the Auditory Verbal Learning 
Test  (AVLT),  Digit  Span  Test  (DST),  Symbol  Digit  Modalities  Test 
(SDMT),  Clock  Drawing  Test  (CDT),  Trail  Making  Test-A  and  Trail 
Making Test-B (TMT-A and TMT-B), Stroop Color and Word Tests A, B 
and C (Stroop A, B and C), and Category Verbal Fluency Test (VFT) was 
used to evaluate the  multiple cognitive domains of episodic memory, 
visual  space,  executive  function,  information  processing  speed,  lan-
guage. In addition, we also evaluated the emotional state of these par-
ticipants  by  utilizing  the  Geriatric  Depression  Scale  (GDS),  Hamilton 
Anxiety Scale (HAMA), and Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD).

2.4. Structural MRI data analysis

Gray  matter  morphology  was  assessed  using  voxel-based 
morphometry (VBM) and surface-based morphometry (SBM) analyses. 
VBM  analysis  was  performed  using  the  CAT12  toolbox  (http://www. 
neuro.uni-jena.de/cat) implemented in Statistical Parametric Mapping 
(SPM12, http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm). First, all the structural T1- 
weighted images were corrected for bias-field inhomogeneities. Second, 
these images were segmented into gray matter, white matter and cere-
brospinal  fluid  density  maps  using  the  “new-segment”  approach 
(Ashburner  and  Friston,  2005),  with  total  intracranial  volume  (TIV) 
obtained.  Third,  a  diffeomorphic  anatomical  registration  through  the 
exponentiated Lie algebra (DARTEL) technique was used to generate a 
custom,  study-specific  template  (Ashburner,  2007).  Fourth,  each  par-
ticipant’s  gray  matter  density  image  was  warped  to  the  customized 
template; then the resultant images were affine registered to the Mon-
treal Neurological Institute (MNI) space and resampled to a voxel size of 
1.5  mm  × 1.5  mm  × 1.5  mm.  Fifth,  the  modulation  was  applied  by 
multiplying  the  transformed  gray  matter  density  maps  with  the 
non-linear  components  of  Jacobian  determinants,  generating  the 
normalized gray matter volume (GMV) maps. An automated anatomical 
labeling (AAL) template was employed to segment the cerebrum into 90 
cortical and subcortical regions (Tzourio-Mazoyer et al., 2002). Mean 
GMV within each brain region were extracted for subsequent analysis.
SBM analysis was performed using the surface-preprocessing pipe-
line of the CAT12 toolbox. We used a projection-based thickness esti-
mation  that  allows  the  calculation  of  cortical  thickness  (CT)  and  the 
central surface (Dahnke et al., 2013). For the estimation of white matter 
distances, we subjected the T1-weighted images to tissue segmentation. 
Local maxima were then projected to other gray matter voxels by using a 
neighbor  relationship  described  by  the  white  matter  distance.  These 
values equal CT. This projection-based approach also included partial 
volume correction and correction for sulcal blurring and asymmetries. 
Topological  correction  was  carried  out  through  a  method  based  on 
spherical harmonics. For reparametrization of the surfaces, an algorithm 
for spherical mapping of the cortical surface was applied (Yotter et al., 
2011).  An  adapted  two-dimensional  DARTEL  algorithm  (Ashburner, 
2007)  was  then  applied  to  the  surface  for  spherical  registration. 
Desikan-Killiany atlas was utilized to parcellate the cerebral cortex into 
68 regions. Mean CT within each cortical region was extracted.

2. 5 fMRI data analysis

Resting-state  BOLD  data  were  preprocessed  using  the  SPM12  and 
Data Processing & Analysis for Brain Imaging (DPABI, http://rfmri.org/ 
dpabi) (Yan et al., 2016) according to a validated pipeline (Cheng et al., 
2024; Mo et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). The first 10 volumes for each 
participant were removed to allow the signal to reach equilibrium and 
the participants to adapt to the scanning noise. The remaining volumes 
were  corrected  for  the  acquisition  time  delay  between  slices.  Then, 
realignment was performed to correct the motion between time points. 
Head motion parameters were computed by estimating the translation in 
each direction and the angular rotation on each axis for each volume. All 
BOLD data were within the defined motion thresholds (i.e., translational 
◦
or rotational motion parameters < 2.0 mm or 2.0 
). We also calculated 
frame-wise  displacement  (FD),  which  indexes  the  volume-to-volume 
changes in head position. Several nuisance covariates (the linear drift, 
the  estimated  motion  parameters  based  on  the  Friston-24  model,  the 
spike volumes with FD > 0.5 mm, the white matter signal, and the ce-
rebrospinal fluid signal) were regressed out from the data. Notably, we 
did not perform global signal regression since it is still a controversial 
topic in resting-state fMRI analysis (Murphy and Fox, 2017). The data-
sets were then band-pass filtered on a frequency range of 0.01 – 0.1 Hz. 
For  spatial  normalization,  individual  structural  images  were  firstly 
co-registered  with  the  mean  functional  images;  then  the  transformed 
structural  images  were  segmented  and  normalized  to  the  MNI  space 

using the DARTEL technique (Ashburner, 2007). Finally, each filtered 
functional volume was spatially normalized to the MNI space using the 
deformation parameters estimated during the above step and resampled 
into a 3-mm cubic voxel.

Two  resting-state  fMRI  measures  reflecting  spontaneous  intrinsic 
brain activity were calculated in the following way. (i) The BOLD time 
course of each voxel obtained from the preprocessed fMRI data without 
band-pass filtering was transformed to a frequency domain via a Fast 
Fourier  Transform,  and  then  the  power  spectrum  was  obtained.  The 
fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (fALFF) was defined 
as the ratio of the power spectrum in the low-frequency band (0.01 - 0.1 
Hz)  to  that  in  the  entire  frequency  range  (Zou  et  al.,  2008).  (ii)  The 
regional homogeneity (ReHo) was calculated as the Kendall’s coefficient 
of  concordance  of  the  time  course  of  a  given  voxel  with  those  of  its 
nearest neighbors (26 voxels) (Yang et al., 2020; Zang et al., 2004). For 
both fALFF and ReHo, the value of each voxel was divided by the global 
mean value within the whole brain, yielding the standardized fALFF and 
ReHo maps. Mean fALFF and ReHo within each brain region of the AAL 
template were extracted.

2.6. DTI data analysis

For DTI data, standard processing steps were performed by using the 
FMRIB  Software  Library  (FSL,  www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl).  First,  eddy 
current  distortion  and  head  motion  were  corrected by  registering  the 
diffusion-weighted  images  to  the  first  b0  image  through  the  affine 
transformations.  Second,  the  data  were  skull-stripped  by  using  the 
FMRIB  Brain  Extraction  Tool.  Finally,  diffusion  parameters  including 
fractional anisotropy (FA), axial diffusivity (AD), and radial diffusivity 
(RD)  were  calculated  to  evaluate  white  matter  integrity  by  using  the 
DTIFIT toolbox. Then, the tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS) pipeline 
was conducted (Smith et al., 2006). Briefly, individual FA images were 
firstly  non-linearly  registered  to  the  MNI  space.  After  transformation 
into the MNI space, mean FA image was created and thinned to generate 
a mean FA skeleton. Then, each subject’s FA image was projected onto 
the skeleton via filling the mean FA skeleton with FA values from the 
nearest  relevant  tract  center  by  searching  perpendicular  to  the  local 
skeleton structure for maximum FA value. Finally, the registration and 
projection information derived from the FA analysis was applied to the 
other  diffusion  parameters  to  project  AD  and  RD  images  onto  this 
common  skeleton.  The  Johns  Hopkins  University  (JHU)  probabilistic 
white matter atlas was used to define 50 white matter tracts in the whole 
brain (Hua et al., 2008). Mean FA, AD, and RD within each tract were 
extracted from the skeletonized diffusion parameter maps.

2.7. Analyses of brain functional and structural networks

In addition to the above-described regional measures, we examined 
global  graph  theory  measures  of  brain  functional  and  structural  net-
works derived from resting-state fMRI and DTI data, respectively. Nodes 
and edges are two basic elements of a brain network. 90 brain regions of 
the AAL template were used to define the nodes of brain networks. To 
define  the  edges  of  functional  networks,  we  computed  the  Pearson’s 
correlation coefficients between the time courses of all possible pairs of 
nodes, resulting in a 90 × 90 functional connectivity matrix for each 
participant. To ensure a small-world topology with a sparse property, 
each  functional  connectivity  matrix  was  thresholded  using  a  sparsity 
threshold range of 0.10 to 0.34 with an interval of 0.01 (Lei et al., 2015; 
Suo et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2011; Zhu et al., 2016), and converted into 
a  binary  matrix.  To  construct  structural  networks,  a  deterministic 
streamline tracking algorithm was performed to obtain the whole-brain 
fiber tractography, with the tracking starting from the deep white matter 
◦
regions and terminating at a voxel with a turning angle greater than 45 
or  with  an  FA  < 0.2.  To  define  the  edges  of  structural  networks,  we 
calculated the number of fibers between any pairs of nodes, yielding a 90 
× 90 fiber number matrix for each participant. To balance the sensitivity

Several global network topological measures based on graph theory 
were  calculated  using  the  GRETNA  software  (http://www.nitrc.org/ 
projects/gretna) (Wang et al., 2015), including small-world attributes 
and network efficiency. Small-world attributes included clustering co-
efficient (Cp), characteristic path length (Lp), normalized clustering co-
efficient (Gamma), normalized characteristic path length (Lambda), and 
small-worldness  (Sigma)  (Watts  and  Strogatz,  1998).  Cp  and  Gamma 
assess  the  extent  of  the  local  density  or  cliquishness  of  the  network, 
which  characterizes  network  segregation.  Lp  and  Lambda  assess  the 
extent  of  average  connectivity  or  overall  routing  efficiency  of  the 
network, which characterizes network integration. Sigma is defined as 
Gamma/Lambda.  Network  efficiency  was  calculated  at  the  local  and 
global levels (Achard and Bullmore, 2007; Latora and Marchiori, 2001). 
Global efficiency (Eglobal) represents the capability of parallel informa-
tion transfer over the network. Local efficiency (Elocal) reflects the fault 
tolerance of the network, which indicates how well the information is 
communicated within the neighbors of a given node when this node is 
eliminated.

2.9. Gut microbiomics analysis

Gut  microbiome  was  quantified  using  16S  rRNA  gene  amplicon 
sequencing.  Briefly,  total  genome  DNA  from  the  fecal  samples  was 
extracted using Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method. To 
construct  the  Polymerase  Chain  Reaction  (PCR)-based  16S  rDNA 
amplicon library for sequencing, PCR enrichment of the V4 hypervari-
able region of 16S rDNA was performed with the forward primer 515F 
(5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′)  and  reverse  primer  806R  (5′- 
GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). Sequencing libraries were generated 
using TruSeq® DNA PCR-Free Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA) 
following  manufacturer’s  recommendations  and  index  codes  were 
added. The library quality was assessed on the Qubit@2.0 Fluorometer 
(Thermo Scientific) and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. At last, the 
library  was  sequenced  on  an  Illumina  NovaSeq  platform  and  250  bp 
paired-end reads were generated.

We used a standard pipeline for 16S rDNA data analysis (Liu et al., 
2020b). First, the raw paired-end reads were assigned to samples based 
on  their  unique  barcode  sequences.  Then,  the  paired-end  reads  were 
merged  to  obtain  raw  tags  based  on  the  3′  overlapping  regions,  and 
barcode  and primers were removed. Quality filtering on the raw tags 
was performed, and kept reads error rates < 1 %. Next, denoising was 
done  by  the  unoise3  command,  which  is  an  implementation  of  the 
UNOISE  algorithm.  After  dereplication,  denoised  sequences  were 
generated and referred to as amplicon sequence variants (ASV). Finally, 
reference-based chimera detection was conducted using the Ribosomal 
Database  Project  (RDP)  as  a  reference  database  (http://rdp.cme.msu. 
edu),  and  then  the  ASV  table  was  generated  by  quantifying  the 
normalized  ASV  counts  in  each  sample.  The  ASV  were  classified  at 
different taxonomical units from the genus to the phylum level, with a 
minimum  confidence  threshold  of  0.1.  After  removing  plastid  and 
non-bacteria, microbial relative abundance at each taxonomic level was 
obtained.

performance  liquid  chromatography  coupled  with  tandem  mass  spec-
trometry  (UHPLC-MS/MS)  system  (Barri  and  Dragsted,  2013;  Want 
et  al.,  2006).  A  pooled  quality  control  (QC)  sample  and  instrument 
blanks  were  used  to  assess  the  reproducibility  and  to  filter  out  the 
chemical background contamination. The raw data were processed by 
MS-DIAL (version 4.92) (Tsugawa et al., 2015) with a mass tolerance of 
0.01 and 0.025 Da for MS1 and MS2, respectively; the minimum peak 
height was 50000, and the identification score cut-off was 70 %. For the 
metabolite identification process, we used the spectral libraries provided 
by  the  MS-DIAL  team,  which  incorporate  all  the  available  public  re-
positories. The average QC to blank peak area ratio was calculated, and 
metabolites with a QC/blank peak area ratio < 3 were removed (Gadara 
et al., 2021). Signal reproducibility was tested by calculating the relative 
standard  deviation  (RSD)  of  QC  sample  technical  replicates,  and  me-
tabolites  with  RSD  > 30  %  were  discarded  (Harshfield  et  al.,  2022). 
Metabolites with nonzero measurement in at least 80 % of the samples 
were included (Bijlsma et al., 2006). Missing values were replaced by 
1/5  of  minimum  positive  values  of  their  corresponding  variables. 
Finally, the quantification values of metabolites were normalized by QC 
samples, made more normally distributed with a generalized log trans-
formation, and standardized using z-scores.

2.11. Statistical analysis

With the SPSS 26.0 software (SPSS, Chicago, Ill), we tested differ-
ences in demographic and neuropsychological data between the groups 
using two-sample t-test or chi-squared test, as appropriate. A threshold 
of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (two-sided).

To 

establish 

the  microbiome-metabolome-neuroimaging- 
neuropsychology  relationship,  we  adopted  a  multi-stage  approach. 
First, linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) (Segata et al., 
2011) was used to identify the microbial taxa that exhibited significant 
differences in relative abundance between CSVD patients and HC, with a 
LDA score threshold of 2. Second, we used Spearman’s correlation an-
alyses to test the associations of the differential microbes with fecal and 
plasma  metabolites  in  CSVD  patients,  followed  by  performance  of 
enrichment  analysis  on  the  affected  metabolites  based  on  the  Kyoto 
Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (https://www. 
kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html). Third, we examined the associations be-
tween the affected metabolites and multimodal neuroimaging measures 
using  Spearman’s  (CT,  FA,  AD,  RD,  and  global  structural  network 
measures)  or  partial  Spearman’s  (fALFF,  ReHo,  and  global  functional 
network measures adjusting for FD; GMV adjusting for TIV) correlation 
analyses. Fourth, we investigated the associations between the affected 
neuroimaging measures and neuropsychological variables using Spear-
man’s  or  partial  Spearman’s  correlation  analyses.  For 
the 
above-mentioned  correlation  analyses,  multiple  testing  was  corrected 
using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, with a corrected signifi-
cance level of P < 0.05.

2.12. Validation analysis

Considering  the  potential  impacts  of  vascular  risk  factors  (hyper-
tension,  diabetes,  hyperlipidemia,  smoking,  and  drinking),  BMI,  and 
Fazekas  scores  of  WMH,  we  repeated  the  above  correlation  analyses 
additionally adjusting for these covariates. Second, to further examine 
the  relationships  of  gut  microbiome  and  metabolome  with  WMH 
severity,  we  performed  Spearman’s  correlation  analyses  of  the  differ-
ential microbes and affected metabolites with WMH Fazekas scores in 
CSVD patients.

4. Discussion

By using a combination of microbiomics, metabolomics, multimodal 
MRI and neuropsychological assessment, the current study interrogated 
the microbiota-gut-brain axis in CSVD. Our data showed both depleted 
and  enriched  gut  microbes  in  CSVD  patients  with  WMH.  Among  the 
differential  microbes,  Haemophilus  and  Akkermansia  were  associated 
with a range of metabolites enriched for Aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis 
pathway.  Furthermore,  the  affected  metabolites  were  associated  with 
neuroimaging  measures  involving  gray  matter  morphology,  sponta-
neous intrinsic brain activity, white matter integrity, and global struc-
tural  network  topology,  which  were  in  turn  related  to  cognition  and 
emotion  in  CSVD  patients.  These  findings  suggest  a  gut  microbiome- 
metabolome-brain-behavior  pathway  underlying  the  interplay  be-
tween gut microbiota dysbiosis and CSVD (Fig. 4), providing an inte-
grative framework  to  understand the  role of  the  microbiota-gut-brain 
axis in the pathophysiological mechanisms of CSVD.

We  found  that  the  gut  microbial  composition  alterations  in  CSVD 
patients  were  characterized  by  both  decreased  and  increased  gut  mi-
crobes.  On  the  one  hand,  Coprococcus  is  a  beneficial  commensal

Further enrichment analysis revealed that the affected metabolites were 
enriched for Aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis pathway. Aminoacyl-tRNAs 
are the essential substrates for translation (Sheppard et al., 2008) and 
play a key role in amino acid transport and protein synthesis. Collec-
tively, our results indicate that altered metabolic processes may mediate 
the relationship between increased Akkermansia and CSVD.

Our multimodal MRI data showed that the affected metabolites were 
associated  with  gray  matter  morphology,  spontaneous  intrinsic  brain 
activity, white matter integrity, and global structural network topology. 
Moreover, some neuroimaging measures were in turn related to cogni-
tion and emotion in CSVD patients, with gray matter morphology pri-
marily associated with depression, white matter integrity with multiple 
cognitive domains, and global structural network topology with general 
cognition.  The  current  findings  are  partially  consistent  with  previous 
reports.  For  example,  Zhu  et  al. found  that  individuals  with  WMH 
showed  gray  matter  atrophy  in  the  bilateral  thalamus,  fronto-insular 
cortices,  and  several  parietal-temporal  regions,  and  gray  matter  atro-
phy  in  the  thalamus  and  fronto-insular  cortices  was  associated  with 
cognitive decline (Zhu et al., 2021). By combining DTI and graph theory, 
Tuladhar  and  colleagues  demonstrated  that  a  decline  in  structural 
network  global  efficiency  was  associated  with  cognition  in  CSVD  pa-
tients with severe WMH (Tuladhar et al., 2020). Liu et al. reported that 
white matter integrity was widely impaired in nondemented CSVD pa-
tients, but structural network topology was relatively preserved and was 
associated with cognition (Liu et al., 2020a).

There are several limitations in this study. First, the observational 
and cross-sectional nature of this work makes the results subjected to the 
influence  of  potential  residual  confounders,  and  limits  our  ability  to 
discern between cause and effect. Further experimental studies or clin-
ical trials are warranted to verify these findings and prove the causality. 
Second, our study employed untargeted metabolomics analysis, which 
only  enabled  relative  quantification  of  metabolites.  Targeted  metab-
olomics analysis will be needed to more precisely quantify metabolites. 
Third, we adopted multi-stage univariate correlation analysis to disen-
tangle  the multi-omic  associations due to its simplicity  and interpret-
ability.  Although  multivariate  methods  (e.g.,  canonical  correlation 
analysis  and  partial  least  squares  regression)  and  mediation  analytics 
could provide more comprehensive and powerful insights into complex 
relationships, our small sample size along with the rather large number 
of our multi-omic variables might impede the implementation of these 
updated analytic approaches. Nevertheless, the multi-omic correlations 
identified in our small sample are still important as they could generate a 
number of hypotheses for further testing and confirmation in a larger 
sample. Fourth, we measured fecal and blood metabolites rather than 
those in the cerebrospinal fluid, which may be more directly and closely 
linked to CSVD. However, it is challenging to collect cerebrospinal fluid 
samples. Finally, this study includes only Chinese participants and thus 
the results may not be generalizable to other ethnic populations.

In conclusion, our work used a comprehensive multi-omic profiling 
to  investigate  the  pathophysiological  mechanisms  underlying  the 
interplay between gut microbiota dysbiosis and CSVD in an integrated 
manner. Our data demonstrated a gut microbiome-metabolome-brain- 
behavior pathway through which gut microbiota dysbiosis was linked 
to  CSVD,  highlighting  the  potential  of  targeting  the  microbiota-gut- 
brain axis as a therapeutic strategy in CSVD patients.
